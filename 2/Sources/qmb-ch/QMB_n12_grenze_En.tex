\chapter{Limits of Quantum Computing: RSA, Weyl, and the Resolution Floor}
\label{ch:grenze}

\section{What Quantum Computers Can and Cannot Do}

This chapter draws three limits that the corpus establishes with justification.
They are not speculative restrictions, but
consequences of mathematical theorems or empirical findings.

\section{Limit 1: RSA Factorization and the Arithmetic Carrier}

RSA (Rivest, Shamir, Adleman 1977) relies on the difficulty of
factorizing large semiprimes $N = p\cdot q$. Shor's algorithm
solves the problem in polynomial time --- in principle. The question is what
the geometry parameter $\xi$ can contribute.

The result of the corpus is clear: \textbf{$\xi$ has no function in $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$.}\status{K} The period $r$ of the modular power function
$f(x) = a^x\bmod N$ is determined by $r = \mathrm{lcm}(p-1,q-1)$ ---
an individual arithmetic property of $N$, not a
geometric parameter. Shor's algorithm uses this period structure;
it is a property of the primes, not of the FFGFT geometry.

The tests in Doc.~075 and Doc.~076 use raster parameters $\sigma$
with values such as $1/42$, $1/100$, $1/1000$ for period search methods.
These quantities are numerical sampling widths, not physical
parameters; they must not be equated with $\xi = 4/30000$.
The resonance picture is in principle correct --- period finding is a
spectral problem --- but the generators of the period are the
primes, not an adjustable geometry parameter.

An advantage over classical algorithms must become visible on the
Shor factorization curve: The error correction
threshold must be maintained for relevant key lengths (2048 bit);
the preparation must be executed in superposition; an
RSA-relevant $N$ must be successfully factored. None of this
is realized as of 2026.\status{X}

\section{Limit 2: Weyl Obstruction and Counting Density}

The Weyl law determines how many eigenvalues a compact state space
can have below a bound $\lambda$:
$N(\lambda)\sim C\cdot\lambda^{d/2}$
--- a power law in dimension $d$.\status{K}

The prime counting function $\pi(T)$ and the Riemann zeros grow
on the other hand like $T\ln T$ --- not a power, but quasi-logarithmic. This
means: A finite compact state space (an $n$-qubit register is
such a thing) cannot fully carry such a spectrum.
The Weyl obstruction excludes three geometric approaches to the
Riemann hypothesis:\status{K} no compact geometry can
provide $T\ln T$ growth from a Laplace spectrum; $\xi$ does not shift
the critical line; the $\xi$ ladder does not discretize the
zeros.

Shor's circuit model is digital and not directly affected by the Weyl limit. But a physical quantum computer is a resonance system;
as long as the threshold theorem for relevant scalings is not demonstrated,
it lies within the resonance limits.\status{S}

\section{Limit 3: The Resolution Floor}

The $\xi$ correction to Bell correlations is
$\Delta\mathrm{CHSH}\approx\xi/(2\pi)\approx2\times10^{-5}$ per
measurement.\status{S} That is the resolution floor: Below this threshold, no FFGFT-specific
deviation from standard QM is measurable. All NISQ processors (NISQ =
Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) operate with physical error rates
$\sim10^{-2}$, three orders of magnitude above the $\xi$ signal.

This means for the quantum computer: The $\xi$ corrections to algorithms
--- Bell damping in Shor, modified gate angles through $K_{\mathrm{frak}}$
--- are buried under NISQ noise.\status{K} Neither a slowdown
nor an acceleration of quantum algorithms through $\xi$ is measurable with
current hardware. This is not a failure, but a precision statement.

Future high-precision Bell tests (loophole-free, detection
efficiency $>99\,\%$, optical platform) could reach the limit.
The photonic system (Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}) is the natural
candidate.

\section{What Remains}

Of the four parallel structures between FFGFT and quantum algorithms
remain after this limit-setting:

The translatability (Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert}) is complete and
symmetric. Every QM calculation is formulable in FFGFT; every FFGFT statement
about quantum systems is mappable in QM. This is not a restriction,
but a strength: Practitioners can keep their familiar formalism.

The added value of the FFGFT lies not in different algorithmic results,
but in the origin of the input values: Masses, couplings, and
$\alpha$ from the same geometry that carries the quantum formalism. That
is the structural asymmetry that Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert} has established.
