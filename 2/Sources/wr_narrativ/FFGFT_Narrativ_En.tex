\chapter{Do We Live in a Matrix?}

The question is old. Plato posed it with the allegory of the cave: people
sit chained, see only shadows on the wall, and take these shadows for
reality. Behind them stands the real light. Descartes formulated it as
radical doubt: what if an evil spirit deceives us, what if everything we
perceive is only a deceptively real illusion? And in 2003 Nick Bostrom gave
it a modern, mathematically precise form: if sufficiently advanced
civilizations can and want to run simulations of their ancestors, then we
live with overwhelming probability in such a simulation.

The question is in vogue. Physicists like Max Tegmark argue that the
universe is not only describable by mathematics — it \textit{is} a
mathematical structure. Computer scientists like Konrad Zuse and Stephen
Wolfram suspect that nature computes on discrete rules, similar to a cellular
automaton. Elon Musk said in an interview that the probability of not living
in a simulation is one in billions.

\begin{laierspur}
Imagine you are playing a video game so perfect that from inside you
cannot tell whether it is a game. The physics within would be consistent,
the mathematics would work, everything would follow rules — you would have
no way to distinguish. That is Bostrom's argument.
\end{laierspur}

It is a serious question. And it is wrongly framed.

Not because the answer would be tedious. But because \enquote{simulation} is the
wrong category. A simulation presupposes: someone computes. There is
hardware outside. There is a simulator. The universe would then be an
artifact — made, not being.

What if the universe is not a simulation \textit{but} has a mathematical
structure — deep, necessary, without external instance? What if the question
is not: \textit{who computes us}, but: *what is the structure that nature
cannot be otherwise?*

That is a different question. And it has a different kind of answer.

Tegmark moves in this direction. His \enquote{Mathematical Universe Hypothesis}
states: all mathematical structures exist — our universe is one of them.
This is bold, but vague. It does not explain which structure. It makes no
predictions. It is a philosophical position, not a physical theory.

\begin{laierspur}
Tegmark says: all possible universes exist, and we happen to live in one
of them. This is like saying: all possible melodies exist, and we happen to
hear this one. It does not explain why this melody sounds the way it does.
\end{laierspur}

What this writing presents is a structure. Concrete, derivable, testable.
No simulator, no external instance, no random universe from an ensemble.
Instead: a geometric axiom — a compact four-dimensional torus with a
threefold symmetry, mathematically $T^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ — from which
algebraically follows what must follow. No free parameters. No fits. No
postulate about particles.

And that leads directly to the second question.

\chapter{What Mathematics Owes Nature}

There is an old philosophical question that physicists like to overlook:
When an equation describes nature — what exactly does it describe?

The obvious answer is: reality. But what does that mean more precisely?
Three positions have emerged in the philosophy of science, and they lead to
very different expectations of a physical theory.

The first position is called \textbf{Instrumentalism}: Mathematics is a tool.
An equation is good when it delivers reliable predictions — nothing more,
nothing less. Whether a deeper reality stands behind the equation is not a
scientific question. The instrumentalist does not ask "what is the
electron", he asks \enquote{what does the electron do}.

The second position is called \textbf{Structuralism}: Reality \textit{is} the
mathematical structure. Not the equation describes the world — the equation
\textit{is} the world, in a precise ontological sense. Tegmark's Mathematical
Universe Hypothesis is the most radical variant: all mathematical structures
exist, our universe is one of them.

The third position — let us call it \textbf{geometric constitutionalism} —
is more precise and more modest at the same time. It says: There is a
geometric foundational structure. From it follow algebraic consequences —
necessary, not postulated. These algebraic objects correspond to observable
quantities. But whether these objects \textit{are} what electrons and quarks
\textit{are} — that remains an open question. The theory claims structure,
not essence.

\begin{laierspur}
Imagine you discover that all rivers in a mountain range spring exactly
where geology demands it — no exceptions, no coincidences. That is a deep
insight. But it does not answer what water \enquote{is}. The structural insight and
the essence claim are different levels.
\end{laierspur}

\chapter{The Torus}

Geometry begins with shapes. And the simplest shape worthy of a physical

rectangle and declares: the right edge is identical to the left edge, and
the top edge is identical to the bottom edge. The result is locally flat —
no point of the torus \enquote{knows} that it lies on a torus — but globally closed.

\begin{laierspur}

\textbf{Galois Fields (Finite Fields)}

A Galois field $GF(q)$ is a finite field with exactly $q$ elements. It exists
exactly when $q = p^n$ for a prime $p$ and $n \geq 1$.

$GF(3)$: $\{0, 1, 2\}$ with addition and multiplication modulo 3.
$GF(9)$: Extension of $GF(3)$ by an element i with $i^2 + 1 = 0$ (in
$GF(3)$: $i^2 = 2$).
$GF(27)$: Extension of $GF(3)$ by an element $\alpha$ that satisfies an
irreducible cubic polynomial.

The multiplicative group $GF(q)^* = GF(q)\setminus\{0\}$ is cyclic of order $q-1$.
For $GF(27)^*$: order $26 = 2\times 13$.

\textbf{The Frobenius Automorphism}

On GF(p$^n$), the Frobenius automorphism is defined by:
$\varphi: x \mapsto x^p$

It is a field automorphism — it preserves addition and multiplication.
Its order is n: $\varphi^n$ = id. On $GF(27)$: $\varphi: x \mapsto x^3$, order 3.

The fixed points of $\varphi$ on $GF(27)^*$ are the elements with $x^3 = x$, i.e.
$x(x^2-1) = 0$: the elements $\{+1, -1\}$ (plus 0). These correspond to the
massive sector.

\textbf{Orbits and Three-Orbits}

An orbit of $x$ under $\varphi$ is the set $\{x, \varphi(x), \varphi^2(x), \ldots\}$ until repetition.
On $\mathbb{Z}_{13}$ under $\varphi: x \mapsto 3x \bmod 13$, four three-orbits arise:
$\{1,3,9\}$, $\{2,5,6\}$, $\{4,10,12\}$, $\{7,8,11\}$.

\textbf{The Legendre Symbol}

The Legendre symbol (a/p) for a prime p and integer a with p $\nmid$ a is
defined as:

\[(a/p) = \begin{cases} +1 & a \text{ quadratic residue mod } p \\ -1 & a \text{ quadratic non-residue mod } p \end{cases}\]

For p = 13: The quadratic residues are {1, 3, 4, 9, 10, 12}.

\textbf{The $\mathbb{Z}_3$-Circulant}

A $\mathbb{Z}_3$-circulant is a 3$\times$3 matrix of the form:

C = [[a, b, c], [c, a, b], [b, c, a]]

It is invariant under cyclic permutation of rows. Hermitian $\mathbb{Z}_3$-circulants
(a real) always have the same three eigenvectors:
$\omega^k$ = (1, $\omega^k$, $\omega^{2k}$)/$\sqrt{3}$ for k = 0, 1, 2, where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$.

The three eigenvalues are:
$\lambda$\_k = a + b$\cdot\omega^k$ + c$\cdot\omega^{2k}$

These three eigenvalues correspond to the three lepton masses. Their
calculation from the winding numbers is the core content of Doc. 282.

\textbf{The $T^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ Orbifold}

$T^4$ is the four-dimensional torus: $T^4 = \mathbb{R}^4/\mathbb{Z}^4$ (identification of points
differing by integer vectors).

$\mathbb{Z}_3$ acts on $T^4$ by simultaneous rotation by 120° in two complex planes.
The fixed points of this action — points mapped onto themselves — form a
finite set.

$T^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ is the orbifold: the quotient space in which $\mathbb{Z}_3$-equivalent
points are identified. At the fixed points, $T^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ has a conical
singularity.

The number of fixed points: 9. This follows from the Lefschetz fixed point
theorem applied to the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ action on $T^4$.

\textbf{The Zeta Function of the Torus}

The spectral zeta function of the Laplace operator on $T^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ is:

\[\zeta_{T^4/\mathbb{Z}_3}(s) = L(s,\chi_0) = (1-3^{-s})\,\zeta(s)\]

where $\zeta(s)$ is the Riemann zeta function and $\chi_0$ is the principal character
modulo 3. This L-function has:
- The trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ at s = $-$2, $-$4, $-$6, ...
- The non-trivial Riemann zeros at Re(s) = 1/2 (assumed)
- Orbifold zeros at $s = 2\pi i k/\ln 3$ on $\mathrm{Re}(s) = 0$

This is no additional assumption — it is the compactification condition of
