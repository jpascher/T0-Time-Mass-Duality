\chapter*{Why the Icosahedron: Three, Five, and Non-Commutation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Why the Icosahedron}

\begin{abstract}
FFGFT is a $\mathbb{Z}_3$ theory from the ground up. Yet the lepton masses
contain the golden ratio $\phi$, and $\phi$ is the number of five. This document
explains why the icosahedron is the bridge: its rotation group $A_5$ is the
smallest finite group in which a three and a five coexist without commuting.
From this non-commutation arises a mixing of the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ modes under the
five-fold rotation, and the mixing has one rational component $2/9$ and two
$\phi$-bearing components. The rational one is the Koide phase (Doc.~367), the
$\phi$-bearing ones are the skeleton of the mass ratios (Doc.~368). A single
matrix element of this mixing carries the entire Koide formula. The document
computes nothing new; it narrates what Docs.~285, 293, 367 and 368 mean together.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Observation}

There are three charged leptons. Their masses stand in the ratio
$m_e : m_\mu : m_\tau \approx 1 : 206{.}8 : 3477{.}4$, and since 1981 this
ratio has been known to obey a strikingly simple formula, the Koide formula:
\[
  \sqrt{m_k} = \sqrt{M}\Bigl(1 + \sqrt2\cos\!\Bigl(\theta + \frac{2\pi k}{3}\Bigr)\Bigr),
  \qquad k = 0,1,2.
\]
The scale $M$ is a unit -- it only says how large the masses are overall. The
phase $\theta$ is the actual information: it determines the ratios.
Empirically $\theta = 2/9$ to $10^{-7}$ (Doc.~292). Why $2/9$? Why not $1/4$,
not $0{.}3$? And why the coefficient $\sqrt2$?

The answer leads through a geometric object that at first has nothing to do
with leptons: the icosahedron. The rest of this document explains why.

\section{Where FFGFT Comes From: The Three}

FFGFT begins with a $\mathbb{Z}_3$. Three generations, triality, the circulant
as mass operator. The mode space is $\mathbb{C}^3$; its natural basis is the
Fourier modes
\[
  v_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\,(1,\ \omega^k,\ \omega^{2k}), \qquad k = 0,1,2.
\]
These are the eigenvectors of the cyclic permutation $C_3$. The mode $v_0$ is
the symmetric one; $v_1$ and $v_2$ carry the phases $\omega$ and $\omega^2$. The
electron sits in $v_1$, the muon in $v_2$, the tau in $v_0$ -- this is the
assignment carried by the Koide index $k$.

What this structure alone does \emph{not} deliver is a number. The
$\mathbb{Z}_3$ says there are three modes and how they transform under $C_3$. It
says nothing about masses. The Koide formula needs two inputs -- the coefficient
$\sqrt2$ and the phase $\theta = 2/9$ -- and neither is contained in the
$\mathbb{Z}_3$.

\section{Where $\phi$ Comes From: The Five}

The golden ratio is the number of the pentagon: $\cos 72^\circ = (\phi-1)/2$,
and the diagonals of a regular pentagon stand to its sides in the ratio $\phi$
\EM. There is no five in the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ structure. If $\phi$ appears in the
lepton masses -- Doc.~368 shows $p_1/p_2 = \phi^8$ exactly \BM~-- a five must
come from somewhere.

\section{Why the Icosahedron: Where Three and Five Meet}

The icosahedron has 20 triangular faces and 12 vertices; at each vertex five
triangles meet. Its rotation group $A_5$ has 60 elements: the identity, 15
rotations by $180^\circ$ (edge midpoints), 20 rotations by $120^\circ$ (face
centres), 24 rotations by $72^\circ$ or $144^\circ$ (vertices) \EM. Three and
five are both present.

That is not trivial. The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_{15}$ also contains elements
of order 3 and 5 -- but there they commute. In $A_5$ they \emph{do not
commute}: a rotation by $120^\circ$ followed by one of $72^\circ$ is not the same
as the reverse order. $A_5$ is the smallest group in which this is so.

Concretely: in standard position the vertices of the icosahedron are the cyclic
permutations of $(0,\pm1,\pm\phi)$. The 3-fold axes pass through face centres;
one of them is $(1,1,1)$, and the rotation by $120^\circ$ about this axis is
\emph{exactly} the permutation $C_3$ of FFGFT. The $\mathbb{Z}_3$ of FFGFT is,
without reinterpretation, a subgroup of the icosahedral group (Doc.~285). The
5-fold axes pass through vertices; one vertex is $(0,1,\phi)$, and the rotation
$R_5$ by $72^\circ$ about this axis is the five-fold rotation used in Doc.~293.

\section{Where the Icosahedron Lives: Unrolled, Not Compact}

Up to here it sounds as if the icosahedron were simply \emph{the} structure
of FFGFT. It is not -- and the corpus is very precise about this (Docs.~285,
314, 364). FFGFT has two pictures connected by a lossless coordinate
transformation, and the icosahedron lives in only one of them.

\paragraph{Compact: the torus $T^4$ with the $D_4$ lattice.}
This is the original. The four-torus carries the $D_4$ lattice, and its
triality is the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ of FFGFT -- as a lattice operation, as a
circulant on the mode orbits (Doc.~314). The winding numbers of the T0 ladder
live here. The lattice knows the three. It knows \emph{no} five:
$|\text{Aut}(D_4)| = 1152 = 2^7\cdot3^2$, and $5\nmid1152$. No icosahedron acts
on a $D_4$ lattice (Doc.~364, incompatibility theorem) \BM.

\paragraph{Unrolled: $\mathbb{R}^3\times S^1$.}
Observed space arises when one circle of the torus becomes time through the
measurement process: $T^4\to\mathbb{R}^3\times S^1$ (Doc.~285). On
\emph{this} $\mathbb{R}^3$, $A_5$ acts as rotation group. The $C_3\subset A_5$
is the same three as the triality in the compact picture -- the rotation by
$120^\circ$ about $(1,1,1)$. But the five exists only here. The icosahedron is
the image of the torus geometry in unrolled space, not the torus itself.

\paragraph{How the five nevertheless enters the field.}
Doc.~364 resolves the apparent tension: what matters is \emph{as what} a
symmetry enters -- as an automorphism of lattice vectors or as a phase. The
$72^\circ$ rotation has characteristic polynomial $(x-1)(x^2-(\phi-1)x+1)$;
its trace is $\phi$. Modulo~3, $\phi$ lies in GF$(9)$ and is primitive there
($\phi^4=-1$, order~8). The roots of the quadratic factor lie not in GF$(9)$
but in GF$(81)$, and have order~5 there -- because $5\mid80=|\text{GF}(81)^*|$
\BM. In the lattice, the $72^\circ$ rotation is a vector automorphism, and
$5\nmid1152$. In the field, the same rotation is only its eigenvalue $\zeta_5$,
and $5\mid80$. Geometric incompatibility becomes arithmetic compatibility as
soon as one passes from vectors to phases. Doc.~364 verbatim: this is the
mechanism behind Doc.~293 -- $\theta=2/9$ arises from $R_5$ as a phase, not as
a lattice operation.

\paragraph{Why this is forced.}
In characteristic~3, $x^3-1=(x-1)^3$ \EM: in no GF$(3^k)$ is there a primitive
cube root of unity. The three \emph{cannot} be a phase and must be lattice.
The five \emph{cannot} be lattice ($5\nmid1152$) and must be phase. The prime~3
distributes the two symmetries onto two complementary channels -- not a
choice, but a constraint. The extension GF$(9)\to$GF$(81)$ that carries
$\zeta_5$ and $\phi$ is precisely the doubling $6=3\oplus3'$ of Doc.~285.

\paragraph{What this means for the narrative.}
When non-commutation of three and five is spoken of below, this is meant: the
three as lattice operation in the compact picture, the five as phase in the
unrolled one. They do not meet in geometry -- there they are incompatible --
but in the field GF$(81)$. The icosahedron is the visible form of this
meeting.

\section{What Non-Commutation Produces}

Because $R_5$ does not commute with $C_3$, $R_5$ is \emph{not diagonal} in the
mode basis $v_0,v_1,v_2$. Applying $R_5$ to the electron mode $v_1$, the result
does not land back in $v_1$ but is distributed over all three modes. The moduli
squared of the projections are the redistribution weights (Doc.~293) \BM:
\[
  p_0 = |\langle v_0|R_5|v_1\rangle|^2 = \frac{2}{9}, \qquad
  p_1 = |\langle v_1|R_5|v_1\rangle|^2 = \frac{2+3\phi}{9}, \qquad
  p_2 = |\langle v_2|R_5|v_1\rangle|^2 = \frac{5-3\phi}{9}.
\]
One sees at once: $p_0$ is rational, $p_1$ and $p_2$ contain $\phi$. That is
structure, not accident. The projection onto the symmetric mode $v_0$ is blind
to the five-fold structure -- it only counts how much of the $\mathbb{Z}_3$
mixing arrives, and that is $2/9 = 2/3^2$. The projections onto $v_1$ and $v_2$
see the five and therefore carry $\phi$.

It becomes even clearer at the symmetric mode itself. What $R_5$ makes of $v_0$:
\[
  \Bigl(|\langle v_0|R_5|v_0\rangle|^2,\ |\langle v_0|R_5|v_1\rangle|^2,\
  |\langle v_0|R_5|v_2\rangle|^2\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{5}{9},\ \frac{2}{9},\ \frac{2}{9}\Bigr) \quad \KM
\]
The complete matrix of moduli squared \KM:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l|ccc@{}}
\toprule
$|\langle v_j|R_5|v_k\rangle|^2$ & $k=0$ & $k=1$ & $k=2$ \\
\midrule
$j=0$ & $5/9$ & $2/9$ & $2/9$ \\
$j=1$ & $2/9$ & $(2+3\phi)/9$ & $(5-3\phi)/9$ \\
$j=2$ & $2/9$ & $(5-3\phi)/9$ & $(2+3\phi)/9$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Two things are immediately visible. Wherever $v_0$ is involved, the entry is
$2/9$ or $5/9$ -- rational, without $\phi$. And $\phi$ appears only between
$v_1$ and $v_2$, the two modes that see the five. Every row and every column
sums to~$1$.

The amplitudes of the first row are $(\sqrt5,\sqrt2,\sqrt2)/3$. The five-fold rotation leaves
$\sqrt5/3$ of the symmetric mode in itself and passes $\sqrt2/3$ to each of the
two non-trivial modes. And $5+2+2 = 9 = 3^2$. This is the one row in which the
five of the icosahedron and the three of FFGFT stand side by side. The trace of
$R_5$ is $1 + 2\cos72^\circ = \phi$ \EM~-- the five is in the operator, the three
in the basis, and the product of both is the row $(5,2,2)/9$.

\section{Doc.~367: Why $\theta = p_0$, and How the Whole Koide Formula Follows}

Doc.~293 had noted that $p_0 = 2/9$ coincides with the Koide angle
$\theta = 2/9$, and left the question of the reason open. Doc.~367 gives the
answer: both are the same quotient $2/3^2$, read in different roles. Numerator:
the non-trivial $\mathbb{Z}_3$ modes. Denominator: the order squared, the
dimension of the operator space $\mathbb{C}^3\otimes\mathbb{C}^3$.

The decisive step comes next. The single matrix element
$A = \langle v_0\mid R_5\,v_e\rangle$ has modulus $\sqrt2/3$ and modulus squared
$2/9$. The Koide formula
\[
  a_k = 1 + \sqrt2\cos\!\Bigl(\theta + \frac{2\pi k}{3}\Bigr)
\]
needs exactly these two numbers: $\sqrt2$ as coefficient, $2/9$ as phase. With
$3|A| = \sqrt2$ and $|A|^2 = 2/9$ this becomes \BM:
\[
  a_k = 1 + 3|A|\cos\!\Bigl(|A|^2 + \frac{2\pi k}{3}\Bigr).
\]
No free parameter. The modulus gives the amplitude, the modulus squared the
phase -- Born-rule structure, but here not as interpretation, rather as algebra:
the same complex number, once as $|A|$, once as $|A|^2$. The Koide formula is
thereby fully derived from the question: how much of the five-fold rotation
lands in the symmetric $\mathbb{Z}_3$ mode?

\section{Doc.~368: The $\phi$-Skeleton}

The two other weights carry the five. Their ratios are pure $\phi$-powers \BM:
\[
  \frac{p_1}{p_2} = \phi^8, \qquad \frac{p_0}{p_2} = 2\phi^4, \qquad
  3\sqrt{p_j} \in \{\sqrt2,\ \phi^2,\ \phi^{-2}\}.
\]
And $\phi^8 \approx 46{.}98$ is, up to a Galois factor, the ratio $m_\tau/m_e$:
$74\,\phi^8$ hits to $0{.}03\,\%$, with $\xi$ correction to $0{.}003\,\%$ \KM.
That is the second role of the icosahedron: $p_0$ supplies the phase, $p_1$ and
$p_2$ supply the skeleton of the mass ratios. Both from the same five-fold
rotation, both without $\phi$ having been inserted anywhere.

\section{Doc.~293: Why It Is the Icosahedron and Nothing Else}

Doc.~293 tested whether $2/9$ depends on the axis. Two hundred random axes with
angle $72^\circ$: none yields $2/9$. Angle scan at fixed icosahedral axis: only
$72^\circ$ yields $2/9$. Order series -- rotations of order 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 about
icosahedral axes: only order 5 yields $2/9$ \KM. The number is
icosahedron-specific. It arises because the axis $(0,1,\phi)$ and the angle
$72^\circ$ together stand to the $(1,1,1)$ axis of the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ exactly
as the icosahedral geometry prescribes.

\section{What the Icosahedral $\mathbb{Z}_3$ Is -- and What It Is Not}

A comparison sharpens the concept. In string theory there are orbifolds
$T^6/\mathbb{Z}_3$ whose $\mathbb{Z}_3$ acts as $\omega\cdot\mathbb{1}$ on
$\mathbb{C}^3$ -- all three coordinates receive the same phase. This
$\mathbb{Z}_3$ is \emph{central}: it commutes with every matrix. The icosahedral
$\mathbb{Z}_3$ is $C_3$ with eigenvalues $(1,\omega,\omega^2)$, trace zero,
\emph{not} central.

Both structures count $2/3^2$ -- the orbifold CFT via the sum of twist-field
weights $1/9+1/9$ \EM~(Doc.~367, external parallel). But only the icosahedral
$\mathbb{Z}_3$ can be \emph{mixed} by $R_5$. For the central one,
$\langle v_0|R_5|v_1\rangle$ would vanish, because $R_5$ would then commute with
$\mathbb{Z}_3$ and preserve sectors. The non-commutation that produced the
number above is exactly what distinguishes the icosahedral structure from an
orbifold. $A_5$ has trivial centre; no element of $A_5$ can become
$\omega\cdot\mathbb{1}$ in a faithful representation. The two $\mathbb{Z}_3$'s
are different objects that happen -- or do not happen -- to share the same
counting quantity.

\section{What Remains Open}

Two things \SM.

First, the Galois factors. $\phi^8$ is exact, but $m_\tau/m_e$ is $74\,\phi^8$,
and $74 = 2\cdot37$ with $37\mid|\text{GF}(3^{18})^*|$ comes from the number
theory of the fields GF$(3^n)$ (Doc.~338), not from icosahedral geometry.
Whether both share a common root is not known.

Second, the fractal correction. The step from percent to $10^{-3}$ level is, in
all routes (Doc.~369), the same number in different guise -- measured, not
derived. The Koide route has it hidden in $\cos(2/9)$ and is therefore precise;
the algebraic routes must add it back.

\section{The Short Version}

The icosahedron is the place where three and five do not get along. FFGFT
brings the three; the icosahedron is the smallest structure that adds a five
without the five commuting with the three. From this non-commutation arises a
mixing, and the mixing has one rational component -- $2/9$ -- and two
$\phi$-components. The rational one is the Koide phase. The $\phi$-components
are the skeleton of the masses. A single matrix element of this mixing carries
the entire Koide formula, parameter-free.
