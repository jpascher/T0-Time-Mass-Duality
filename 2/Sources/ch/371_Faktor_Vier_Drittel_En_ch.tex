\begin{abstract}
The numerical factor $4/3$ in the scaling parameter $\xi = \tfrac{4}{3}\times10^{-4}$
has so far been motivated at three places in the corpus: sphere volume (Docs.~006,
205), the Casimir operator of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ and the Casimir effect (Doc.~324).
This document adds two occurrences not previously recorded and orders all five by
their common cause. First: the factor $4/3$ in the electromagnetic mass of the
electron, unexplained since Abraham (1902) and Lorentz (1904), arises because
each of the three field components carries one third of the field energy --- a
consequence of three-dimensionality, not a computational error. Second: the
Casimir values of the two smallest non-abelian groups, $C_2(\mathrm{SU}(2))=3/4$
and $C_2(\mathrm{SU}(3))=4/3$, are reciprocals of each other; $N=2$ is the only
case in which consecutive fundamental representations achieve this. Both values
belong to the electron: $3/4$ is its spin Casimir, $4/3$ its mass factor. The
Pythagorean triple $(3,4,5)$. The two factors $3/4$ and $4/3$ are reciprocals, and
their product is~1. Where does this pair arise naturally? In the simplest
right-angled triangle with integer sides: the triple $(3,4,5)$. It is not one
among many; it is distinguished in four independent ways.

The triple $(3,4,5)$ carries this reciprocal pair as $\tan\alpha=4/3$,
$\cot\alpha=3/4$ and is, by Berggren's theorem, the root of all primitive
triples. The angle $\alpha = \arctan(4/3)$ does not occur among the shortest vectors
of the lattices $D_3$, $D_4$, but it does occur on the shell $|v|^2 = 10$: there
lie $(3,1,0,0)$ and $(1,3,0,0)$ with $\cos\alpha = 3/5$. The cause is
$D_2 = (1+i)\,\mathbb Z[i]$ and $(1+i)(2\pm i) = 1+3i,\ 3+i$: the triple sits,
via the factor $(1+i)$, in the carrier of FFGFT. A further 3D occurrence, the
enthalpy factor $(u+p)/u = 4/3$ for isotropic pressure $p = u/3$, is added.
\end{abstract}

\section{Starting Point in the Corpus}

motivated three times in the corpus:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Casimir operator} (Doc.~324): $C_2(\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\rm fund})
  = \tfrac{9-1}{6} = \tfrac{4}{3}$.
\item \textbf{Casimir effect} (Docs.~324, 009): the effective factor $4/3$ in

\end{enumerate}

These three occurrences stand side by side in the corpus; Doc.~324 calls the
agreement of (2) and (3) ``no coincidence'' without naming the common cause.
This document names it and adds two occurrences.

\section{Electromagnetic Mass: the Factor 4/3 since 1902}
\label{sec:em}

\subsection{The historical finding \EM}

If the electron is modelled as a uniformly charged spherical shell of radius
$a$, the field energy at rest is $U = e^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0 a)$ and the
associated mass is $m_0 = U/c^2$. If instead one computes the field momentum of
the slowly moving shell, one obtains
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf p = \frac{4}{3}\,\frac{U}{c^2}\,\mathbf v ,
  \label{eq:p43}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ a momentum mass of $\tfrac{4}{3}m_0$. This factor first appeared in
Abraham (1902) and Lorentz (1904). Poincaré (1906) introduced non-electromagnetic
binding stresses that remove it computationally; Fermi (1922), Wilson (1936) and
Rohrlich (1960) showed that a covariant definition of the four-momentum makes it
vanish as well. The literature still disagrees on whether this explains the
\emph{cause} or merely redefines the \emph{symptom} (Singal 2024; Universe
11(3):97, 2025).

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Why clarification around 1900 was so difficult.}
The confusion had three mutually reinforcing sources.

\emph{First: there was no special relativity.} Abraham and Lorentz worked
in a framework where energy and momentum were still separate, independent
quantities. Without $E = mc^2$ and the four-momentum, a factor $4/3$
between momentum and energy looks like a genuine contradiction --- not like
an artefact of a wrong separation.

\emph{Second: the electron was new and its model undecided.} Thomson had
discovered the electron in 1897. Whether it was a rigid sphere, a deformable
shell, or a point mass was an open question. Within Lorentz's sphere model,
the factor $4/3$ was real and troublesome.

\emph{Third: Poincaré's solution was the wrong solution.} The
``Poincaré pressure'' cancelled the factor computationally, but it was
an ad-hoc assumption without physical foundation --- and it obscured the
actual cause.

After Einstein (1905) and Minkowski (1908) the problem could have been
resolved cleanly: the factor $4/3$ arises only when $E$ and $\mathbf p$
are integrated separately from the field, without accounting for the
boundary terms of Maxwell's stress tensor. Rohrlich (1960) made this clear.
What was never the subject of this debate: that $4/3 = 2(1-\tfrac{1}{3})$
is a geometric statement about the three-dimensionality of space. The
question asked was \emph{``How do we get rid of the factor?''} --- rather
than \emph{``Where does it really come from?''}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Where the factor comes from \EM}

The calculation is short and exposes the cause. With $\mathbf B =
\mathbf v\times\mathbf E/c^2$ to first order, the momentum density is
$\mathbf g = \varepsilon_0\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf p = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{c^2}\int
  \bigl[\mathbf v\,E^2 - \mathbf E\,(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v)\bigr]\,dV
  = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{c^2}\,\mathbf v \int \bigl(E^2 - E_\parallel^2\bigr)\,dV .
\end{equation}
For a spherically symmetric field, $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = 1/3$, hence
$\int E_\parallel^2\,dV = \tfrac13\int E^2\,dV$, and with
$U = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,dV$ it follows that
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf p = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{c^2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\int E^2\,dV\cdot\mathbf v
  = \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{2U}{c^2}\,\mathbf v = \frac{4}{3}\,\frac{U}{c^2}\,\mathbf v .
\end{equation}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The factor $4/3$ is $2\cdot(1-\tfrac13)$.} It arises because in
three-dimensional space each field component carries one third of the energy,
and the component parallel to the motion does not contribute to the momentum.
This is the same one-third split that sits in the sphere volume $V = \tfrac13\,r\,S$
(Archimedes). The factor is not an artefact of a wrong definition but a
statement about the dimension of space.
\end{quote}

a geometric constant at the root. This is a structural agreement \SM; a

\section{Casimir Values: 3/4 and 4/3 as a Reciprocal Pair}
\label{sec:casimir}

\subsection{The calculation \EM}

The quadratic Casimir operator measures the total charge of a representation: it
is the group-theoretic analogue of $j(j+1)$ for angular momentum. For
$\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ it is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}+1)
= \tfrac{3}{4}$ --- the quantum spin number of the electron. For $\mathrm{SU}(3)$
in the fundamental representation (the three colour charges of quarks) it is
$\tfrac{4}{3}$ --- the same factor as in the electromagnetic mass.

The quadratic Casimir operator of the fundamental representation of
$\mathrm{SU}(N)$ is $C_2 = \tfrac{N^2-1}{2N}$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Group & $C_2$ & physical location \\
\midrule
$\mathrm{SU}(2)$ & $3/4$ & spin $\tfrac12$: $s(s+1) = \tfrac34$ \\
$\mathrm{SU}(3)$ & $4/3$ & colour charge; Doc.~324 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
We have $C_2(\mathrm{SU}(2))\cdot C_2(\mathrm{SU}(3)) = 1$. The verification
script shows \BM: among all $N\ge2$, $N=2$ is the only case in which
$C_2(N)\cdot C_2(N{+}1)=1$. The two smallest non-abelian groups form a
distinguished reciprocal pair.

\subsection{Both values belong to the electron \SM}

The value $3/4$ is the spin Casimir of the electron. The value $4/3$ is, by
§\ref{sec:em}, its electromagnetic mass factor and, in FFGFT, the numerator of
its mass reciprocal $1/\xi$. Spin and mass of the electron thus carry the
reciprocal pair $(3/4,\,4/3)$. The fundamental formula $\tilde T\cdot m = 1$ is
a reciprocal structure; that the two electron invariants also form a reciprocal
pair is structurally compatible, but not derived.

\section{The Triple (3,4,5)}
\label{sec:345}

The reciprocal pair $(3/4,\,4/3)$ is the leg ratio of the smallest Pythagorean
triple: $\tan\alpha = 4/3$, $\cot\alpha = 3/4$, $\alpha = \arctan\tfrac43
\approx 53.13^\circ$, $\sin\alpha = \tfrac45$, $\cos\alpha = \tfrac35$. The
triple is not one among many; four properties distinguish it \EM, all
recomputed in the verification script \BM:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Berggren root (1934).} Every primitive Pythagorean triple arises
  from $(3,4,5)$ by a unique sequence of multiplications with three integer
  $3\times3$ matrices. The three children of $(3,4,5)$ are $(5,12,13)$,
  $(21,20,29)$, $(15,8,17)$; $(3,4,5)$ itself has no positive parent triple.
  It is the root of the tree.
\item \textbf{Arithmetic progression.} $(3,4,5)$ is the only right triangle
  whose sides form an arithmetic progression:
  $a^2+(a+d)^2=(a+2d)^2 \Leftrightarrow (a-3d)(a+d)=0$.
\item \textbf{Inradius 1.} $r = (3+4-5)/2 = 1$. The triples $(5,12,13)$ and
  $(8,15,17)$ have $r=2$ and $r=3$; $(3,4,5)$ is the unit step.
\item \textbf{Gaussian integers.} $(2+i)^2 = 3+4i$. The triple is the square
  of the smallest non-trivial Gaussian integer; $\alpha$ is the angle of this
  squaring.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Five in the Triple and Its Connection to the Icosahedral Group \SM}

The hypotenuse of the triple is~5, and~5 appears in the trigonometric
values: $\sin\alpha = 4/5$, $\cos\alpha = 3/5$. This~5 is not accidentally
the same as the~5 in $A_5$.

The algebraic chain is as follows. The triple arises as the square of the
Gaussian integer $2+i$: $(2+i)^2 = 3+4i$ \BM. The Gaussian norm is
$|2+i|^2 = 4+1 = 5$. This number~5 is prime in $\mathbb Z[i]$: it splits as
$5 = (2+i)(2-i)$, two conjugate Gaussian prime factors. The same prime~5
appears in the icosahedral group. The group $A_5$ has order~60, and the
fivefold rotation $R_5$ has order~5. Its eigenvalue $\zeta_5$ (fifth root of
unity) lives in the finite field $\mathrm{GF}(81) = \mathrm{GF}(3^4)$, because
$5 \mid 80 = |\mathrm{GF}(81)^*|$ \BM. In the same field $\mathrm{GF}(81)$,
via the subfield $\mathrm{GF}(9) \subset \mathrm{GF}(81)$, also lives the
golden ratio $\phi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$ (Docs.~364, 370).

The connection between the triple $(3,4,5)$ and the icosahedral group $A_5$
thus runs through the prime~5 and the field $\mathrm{GF}(81)$:
\[
  (2+i)^2 = 3+4i \;\Longrightarrow\; |2+i|^2 = 5 \;\Longrightarrow\;
  5 \mid |\mathrm{GF}(81)^*| = 80 \;\Longrightarrow\; \zeta_5 \in \mathrm{GF}(81).
\]
This connects the triple algebraically with the icosahedral part of the corpus
(Docs.~285, 293, 367, 370). What this connection is not: a geometric
identification. The angle $\alpha = \arctan(4/3) = 53.13^\circ$ is not a
rotation angle of $A_5$ (which has $72^\circ$, $144^\circ$, $120^\circ$,
$180^\circ$). The connection is algebraic, not geometric \SM.

\subsection{The angle $\alpha$ in the D lattice \BM}
\label{sec:winkel}

The $D_n$ lattice is what crystallography calls the face-centred cubic (FCC)
lattice: in $\mathbb R^n$ all integer vectors whose coordinate sum is even.
In FFGFT, $D_4$ is the carrier lattice of the torus $T^4$; its shortest
vectors ($|v|^2=2$) define its lattice constant. The question is: on which
shells does the angle $\arctan\tfrac43 = 53.13^\circ$ appear?

Among the shortest vectors ($|v|^2=2$) of the $D_3$ lattice (FCC) and the $D_4$
lattice, $\alpha = \arctan\tfrac43 = 53.13^\circ$ does not occur; both yield
only $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $120^\circ$ \XM. On the shell $|v|^2 = 10$ it
does occur: $(3,1,0,0)$ and $(1,3,0,0)$ lie in $D_4$ (even coordinate sum), and
$\cos\angle = 6/10 = 3/5$ \BM.

The cause is algebraic. The two-dimensional lattice $D_2$ (even coordinate sum)
is exactly the ideal $(1+i)\,\mathbb Z[i]$ of the Gaussian integers \BM, and
\begin{equation}
  (1+i)(2+i) = 1+3i, \qquad (1+i)(2-i) = 3+i,
\end{equation}
with $|1+3i|^2 = 10$. The angle between $2+i$ and $2-i$ is $\arccos\tfrac35
= \alpha$; multiplication by $(1+i)$ rotates both by $45^\circ$ and leaves
the angle unchanged. The triple $(3,4,5)$, which by §\ref{sec:345} is the
square of $2+i$, therefore sits, via the factor $(1+i)$ --- the generator of
the D lattice --- in the carrier geometry of FFGFT, on the first shell whose
norm is not composed purely of $|1+i|^2=2$ and $|2|^2=4$.

The Berggren matrices are isometries of the quadratic form $x^2+y^2-z^2$, i.e.\
elements of the integer Lorentz group $O(2,1;\mathbb Z)$ (Cha and Conceição
2023) \EM; the tree of all triples is thus a discrete hyperbolic structure on
the same lattice.


\subsection{The Right Angle in FFGFT --- a Further Thread \SM}

That the 90° angle plays the key role in \S\ref{sec:em} has not yet been
named explicitly in the text. The derivation shows it: the field component
\emph{parallel} to the direction of motion ($0^\circ$) does not contribute to
the field momentum; the two components \emph{perpendicular} to it ($90^\circ$)
do. In three dimensions with three orthogonal directions, one falls out and two
remain. That is $2/3$. Multiplied by~2 (from the cross-product structure of
$\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$) gives $4/3$. The geometric statement is:
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac43 = 2 \cdot \bigl(1 - \tfrac13\bigr) = 2 \cdot
  \tfrac{\text{number of directions perpendicular to motion}}{3}.
\end{equation}
The 90° angle is not incidental but the cause. It also appears explicitly in the
calculation: $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = 1/3$ follows from spherical symmetry,
because this average over all angles is precisely what eliminates the right angle
as a mean.

\paragraph{The right angle in the D$_4$ lattice.}
Among the shortest vectors of $D_4$ (even coordinate sum, $|v|^2 = 2$)
exactly the angles $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $120^\circ$, $180^\circ$ appear
\BM. The $90^\circ$ is thus a distinguished lattice angle, but it is not alone:
it shares the space of lattice angles with $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$. Whether
the $90^\circ$ lattice angle plays a distinguished role in FFGFT beyond the
others --- for instance as the separation angle between the four torus
directions, as an orthogonality condition between space and time, or as a
condition for the projection $T^4 \to \mathbb R^3 \times S^1$ --- has not been
investigated. That is the genuinely open question.

\paragraph{The Fourier modes are orthogonal.}
The three lepton modes $v_0, v_1, v_2$ form an orthonormal basis:
$\langle v_j | v_k\rangle = \delta_{jk}$ \BM. Each pair of modes stands at
$90^\circ$ in Hilbert space. This orthogonality is the basic condition for the
matrix element $\langle v_0 | R_5 | v_1\rangle$ to measure the mixing between
two \emph{perpendicular} states --- and for the modulus squared
$|\langle v_0|R_5|v_1\rangle|^2 = 2/9$ to be a transition probability in the
sense of Born's rule. Without $90^\circ$ in Hilbert space, no clean probability
interpretation.

\paragraph{2/3 and 4/3 as a bridge to the Koide quotient.}
The Koide quotient is $Q = (\sum m_k)/(\sum\sqrt{m_k})^2 = 2/3$ \EM.
The field-momentum formula from \S\ref{sec:em} gives
$\mathbf p = \tfrac{4}{3}(U/c^2)\mathbf v$.
Numerically, exactly:
\[
  \tfrac{4}{3} = 2 \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} = 2\,Q .
\]
The factor $4/3$ of the electromagnetic mass is twice the Koide quotient.
This is a numerical identity --- whether a deeper connection lies behind it,
between the three-dimensionality factor of field physics and the mass-structure
quotient of the leptons, is open \SM. It would be the most direct link of
the Abraham-Lorentz problem to the lepton masses that the corpus currently
contains: via the number $2/3$, which appears on the one hand as the Koide
quotient, and on the other as the geometric fraction of transverse directions
in three-dimensional space.

\paragraph{The $T^4$ torus and the four orthogonal circles.}
The torus $T^4$ in FFGFT carries four circle directions that in the standard
setup stand pairwise at $90^\circ$ --- the simplest metric on the product torus
$S^1\times S^1\times S^1\times S^1$. These four $90^\circ$ pairs generate the
$D_4$ lattice of winding numbers. The transition to spacetime
$T^4 \to \mathbb R^3\times S^1$ picks \emph{one} direction (the mass dimension
that becomes time) and leaves the other three as spatial directions, pairwise at
$90^\circ$. The three-dimensionality of space --- that exactly \emph{three}
directions can stand perpendicular to a motion --- is therefore not a free
choice but follows from this projection step. The $4/3$ factor is in this
picture the signature of the symmetry that was left behind: the original
four-dimensionality of the torus leaves in three-dimensional space exactly the
three orthogonal directions that produce $4/3 = 2\cdot(1-1/3)$.

Whether this reading --- $4/3$ as a trace of the projection $T^4\to\mathbb R^3\times S^1$
--- can be formally carried out in the corpus is the sharpest open question of
this subsection \SM.
