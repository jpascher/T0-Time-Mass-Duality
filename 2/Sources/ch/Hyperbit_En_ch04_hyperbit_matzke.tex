\section{The Starting Point: A Sign}

Matzke begins with $e_i^2 = +1$. That is a \textbf{bit} --- something that
yields one when squared. No space, no time, no metric. Only the
statement: there are distinguishable things. \status{SETZUNG}

\begin{laierspur}
Imagine you know only one thing: There are things that differ.
Nothing more. No space, no force, no matter --- only distinguishability.
Matzke's hyperbit is exactly that: the mathematical object that carries precisely this
statement, and nothing more.
\end{laierspur}

\begin{matzbox}[Matzke: Axiom and Bridges]
\status{SETZUNG} Single axiom: $e_i^2 = +1$ (Hyperbit).\\[4pt]
Physical units via three external bridges:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Bekenstein: $4\lP^2$ per bit (entropy-area relation)
  \item Compton $=$ Schwarzschild: $\lP$ as length scale
  \item Landauer-Einstein: $\mbit = \mP \ln 2 / (2\pi)$
\end{itemize}
\end{matzbox}

\section{Why Cl(6) --- and Why Three Colors}

If one takes several $e_i$ and lets them anticommute, a structure with
dimension, orientation and rotations emerges from nothing. $\mathrm{Cl}(6)$
decomposes into exactly three Witt pairs --- three packages each consisting of one creator
and one annihilator. \status{B}

According to Furey, this is exactly the structure of a fermion generation: three colors,
one lepton. The three is not chosen, it falls out.
\textbf{Geometry as bookkeeping over distinguishability} --- not as
a presupposition.

\begin{laierspur}
You take three coins and place them so that none touches the other.
Already you have a triangular structure --- not because you drew a triangle,
but because the coins force it. Likewise: The Clifford algebra
$\mathrm{Cl}(6)$ generates threefold structure not because one builds it in,
but because anticommutativity plus six generators leaves nothing else.
\end{laierspur}

\section{The Black Hole Part}

Bekenstein: A black hole has entropy proportional to the area, one
bit per four Planck areas. Matzke reverses: If a bit is an area,
then a collection of bits is a volume. He sets the Compton wavelength
equal to the Schwarzschild radius and obtains the Schwarzschild radius
\emph{without ever using the Einstein equations}. \status{B}

For $20\,M_\odot$: $59{,}1$\,km. Exactly correct.

The stability threshold is at $6{,}41$ bits. $\mathrm{Cl}(6)$ has $6{,}000$
bits. The universe consists of fermions that sit exactly $0{,}41$ bits below
the collapse threshold.

\begin{laierspur}
Imagine you stack Lego bricks. Above a certain height,
the tower collapses under its own weight. In Matzke's framework,
a system of bits collapses into a black hole above a certain bit count.
The critical number: 6.41. A fermion has 6.00 bits ---
just barely below.
\end{laierspur}

\section{Where the Elegance is Bought}

The $6{,}41$ depends on a stipulation: To get from bits to masses,
Matzke needs a conversion rate. He takes Landauer ($E = k_BT\ln 2$)
and evaluates at Planck temperature:
\[
  \mbit = \frac{\mP \ln 2}{2\pi} \approx 0{,}11\,\mP
\]
A \emph{universal} bit mass. \status{SETZUNG}

Only: Landauer is a statement about thermal carrier ensembles, not
about abstract information (A271--A273). And the Planck temperature is
not the temperature of any physical system --- it is an
energy scale. The choice $T = T_P$ is a stipulation that does not justify
itself.

\begin{keyresult}[Shortest Comparison]
Matzke's algebra is beautiful because it shows how far one gets with pure
combinatorics. It is \emph{simple} because it stops asking at three points
--- three borrowed bridges.\\[4pt]
FFGFT pays the price upfront ($\xi$ derive, $L_0$, justify the torus)
and then needs no bridges. Both calculations
work out --- they look elegant at different points.
\end{keyresult}
