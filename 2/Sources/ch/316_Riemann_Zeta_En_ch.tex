\section*{Preliminary: the answer in short}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Preliminary: the answer in short}

\begin{keyresult}[Result]
The Riemann hypothesis is an \textbf{arithmetic} matter. The torus and
$\xi$ contribute nothing to its solution. The result of this document is
a \emph{negative} one -- three obvious geometric approaches are excluded
with reasons given.
\end{keyresult}

The blanket formulation would be too coarse in one respect, hence the
refinement.

\textbf{What remains purely a matter of numbers.} Where the zeros lie,
how they stand relative to one another, how one grasps them
individually -- that is carried by arithmetic, by the primes. Three
geometric approaches were tested and all are negative: no compact
geometry can carry the zeros as its spectrum (Section~\ref{sec:weyl}, at
theorem level), $\xi$ does not shift the critical line
(Section~\ref{sec:casimir}), and the $\xi$ ladder discretises nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:rand}, out-of-sample test negative).

\textbf{What is nevertheless not merely a matter of numbers.} Two
things. First, the Riemann zeta is a \emph{factor} in the spectrum of
the $\mathbb{Z}^4$ torus -- an identity, not a picture
(Section~\ref{sec:faktor}). The Riemann hypothesis is thereby a
statement about a geometric object of the framework; only one that the
geometry cannot answer. Second, the form of the zero density says
\emph{where} the question belongs: $N(T)$ grows like $T\ln T$, and no
Laplacian on any compact manifold can deliver that -- it requires a
dilation structure, hence the unrolled domain. The geometry indicates
the address without supplying the answer.

\textbf{The striking thing is precisely that both occur together:} the
numbers demonstrably sit in the torus spectrum, but the torus cannot
generate them.

\textbf{The yield.} The dead ends are closed, with reasons rather than
conjecture -- and four positive findings stand alongside them: two
identities of the framework (the factorisation of the torus spectral
zeta and the symmetry position of the Casimir interval) and two
measurements on the prime structure (the bicoherence-measured
cross-term situation and its physical evidence in the harmonic
series).

\section{Starting point and claim}

\textbf{[Q]} The corpus mentions the Riemann hypothesis in exactly one
place: Doc.~024 notes, in an AI/network context, that the
\enquote{number space} has dimensions \enquote{modulated by the Riemann
hypothesis and $\zeta$ functions}. That is a conceptual marginal remark
without an epistemic marker and without derivation. A worked-out
connection does not exist in the corpus.

\textbf{[K]} At the same time Doc.~314 contains material that touches
the $\zeta$ function directly: Epstein zeta, Casimir point, heat kernel
on $\text{T}^4$. This document examines systematically what follows.

\textbf{Claim.} It proves nothing about the Riemann hypothesis and does
not claim to. Three things are clarified: which connections hold as
identities \textbf{[K]}, which path carries constructively \textbf{[B]},
and which obvious paths are \emph{demonstrably} not passable
\textbf{[X]}. The exclusion is the most valuable part -- it saves work

\section{Two findings that hold as identities}
\label{sec:faktor}

\subsection{The Riemann zeta is a factor of the torus spectral zeta}

\textbf{[K]} Doc.~314 carries the closed form of the $\mathbb{Z}^4$
spectral zeta (verified threefold there; confirmed here against the
direct sum with analytic tail correction to $10^{-7}$ through
$10^{-12}$):
\[
  Z_{\mathbb{Z}^4}(s) \;=\; 8\,\bigl(1 - 4^{1-s}\bigr)\,
  \zeta(s)\,\zeta(s-1).
\]
This is not an analogy but an identity. The zeros of the torus spectral
zeta therefore have three sources:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Source & Location of the zeros \\
\midrule
$\zeta(s) = 0$ & $\Re(s) = 1/2$ (non-trivial -- the RH) \\
$\zeta(s-1) = 0$ & $\Re(s) = 3/2$ (shifted by 1) \\
$1 - 4^{1-s} = 0$ & $s = 1 + 2\pi i k/\ln 4$ (lattice factor) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

On this reading the Riemann hypothesis is a statement about the spectrum
of the $\mathbb{Z}^4$ torus -- about a geometric object of the
framework, not merely about a number series.

\subsection{The Casimir point lies symmetric to the critical line}
\label{sec:casimir}

\textbf{[K]} Doc.~314 computes the $\zeta$-regularised vacuum energy
$E = \pi\,\zeta_M(-1/2)$ and finds the order reversal: D4 minimises the
Epstein zeta for $s > 0$, at $s = 0$ all lattices are equal, and at
$s = -1/2$ the $\mathbb{Z}^4$ torus lies lower ($-0.9321$ against
$-0.8686$).

The Casimir point $s = -1/2$ has, under the functional equation
$\zeta(s) = \chi(s)\zeta(1-s)$, the mirror point $s = 3/2$. The axis of
symmetry:
\[
  \frac{-1/2 + 3/2}{2} \;=\; \frac12
  \qquad\text{-- exactly the critical line.}
\]

The physically occupied interval $[-1/2,\,3/2]$ has the critical line as
its midpoint: consistency between physics and arithmetic.

\textbf{[X]} No proof follows from this -- the critical line is fixed by
the functional equation anyway. Nor can $\xi$ shift it: a perturbation
of order $\xi \sim 10^{-4}$ (even with the RG amplifier $100\xi$) does
not reach an axis fixed by a functional identity.

\section{The decisive obstruction: Weyl against Riemann-von Mangoldt}
\label{sec:weyl}

The obvious move would be to seek an operator whose eigenvalues are the
zeros (Hilbert-P\'olya programme), and to deform
$\text{T}^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ suitably for that purpose. \textbf{This entire
branch is excluded -- at theorem level, not as intuition.}

\textbf{[Q]} \emph{Weyl's law.} For \emph{every} compact Riemannian
manifold of any dimension $d$, asymptotically
$N(\lambda) \sim C_d\,\text{Vol}\,\lambda^{d/2}$ -- a pure power.
Curvature, boundary and orbifold points change only lower orders, not
the leading term.

\textbf{[Q]} \emph{Riemann-von Mangoldt (proved).}
\[
  N(T) = \frac{T}{2\pi}\ln\frac{T}{2\pi} - \frac{T}{2\pi}
  + \frac78 + O(1/T).
\]

\textbf{[K]} The comparison via the local exponent
$\mathrm d\ln N/\mathrm d\ln T$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrr}
\toprule
$T$ & $N(T)$ & local exponent $d/2$ \\
\midrule
$10^{3}$ & $6.48\cdot10^{2}$ & $1.2457$ \\
$10^{5}$ & $1.38\cdot10^{5}$ & $1.1153$ \\
$10^{8}$ & $2.48\cdot10^{8}$ & $1.0642$ \\
$10^{12}$ & $3.95\cdot10^{12}$ & $1.0403$ \\
$10^{20}$ & --- & $1.0233$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The exponent is $1 + 1/\ln(T/2\pi)$ -- it falls monotonically towards 1
but is \emph{never constant}. Range over 17 decades: $0.22$. The best
power fit in the window $[10^3, 10^6]$ deviates systematically by up to
$11\,\%$.

\begin{keyresult}[Exclusion at theorem level]
\textbf{[X]} There is no compact Riemannian manifold -- of whatever
dimension, curvature, topology or orbifold structure -- whose Laplace
spectrum is the Riemann zeros. Reason: $T\ln T$ is not a power
$T^{d/2}$.
\end{keyresult}

\textbf{Consequence.} The branch \enquote{deform
$\text{T}^4/\mathbb{Z}_3$ suitably} is settled completely, not only in
the flat case. The extra $\ln T$ is the signature of a \textbf{scale
degree of freedom}: a dilation direction with logarithmically growing
volume. That is not a Laplacian on a manifold.

\textbf{Not a curvature problem.} What generates logarithmic lengths
is a dilation structure -- the multiplicative group $\mathbb{R}_+^*$ --
not curvature; the Berry-Keating operator $H = xp$ is the generator of
dilation, not of a hyperbolic flow. The Weyl obstruction therefore
applies independently of any metric and renders the curvature question
moot.

\section{The constructive link -- and why it does not carry}
\label{sec:zelle}

If the zero density requires a scale degree of freedom, the responsible
domain is the \textbf{unrolled} one (Doc.~315) -- multiplicative,
logarithmic, with dilation as its natural operation.

\subsection{The approach}

\textbf{[Q]} \emph{Berry-Keating (1999)}~\cite{berry1999}. The semiclassical count of the
dilation generator $H = (xp+px)/2$ in a phase space with a minimal cell:
\[
  N(E) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\bigl[\,E\ln\bigl(E/(l_x l_p)\bigr)
  - E + l_x l_p\,\bigr].
\]
With $l_x l_p = 2\pi$ this becomes exactly the Riemann-von Mangoldt
formula (verified: difference constant $0.125 = 1 - 7/8$, a pure
boundary term, over $T = 15 \ldots 5000$).

The obvious chain would then be: Doc.~314 Ch.~D1 carries
$\lambda_4\cdot m = 2\pi$ as an exact position relation; in the unrolled
domain the quantity conjugate to scale is the dilation momentum; hence
FFGFT supplies the cell condition.

\subsection{Why the chain does not carry}

\textbf{[X] Stage 1 -- the cell condition is empty of content.}
$l_x l_p = 2\pi\hbar$ is, in natural units, the Planck cell, and follows
from the canonical quantisation of \emph{every} conjugate pair. That
$\lambda_4 m = 2\pi$ has the same form is no independent finding -- the
de Broglie relation \emph{is} the statement $\hbar = 1$. Moreover the
count is logarithmically insensitive to the cell (a factor 2 wrong
$\Rightarrow$ $N$ changes by ${\sim}1\,\%$); a \enquote{hit} would not
have been a test in any case.

\textbf{[X] Stage 2 -- the actual content fails quantitatively.}
Berry-Keating requires something sharper than the cell: two
\emph{independent} cutoffs $x > l_x$ and $p > l_p$ whose product is the
Planck cell. The FFGFT double cutoff makes this testable:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Quantity & Value \\
\midrule
UV: $L_0 = \xi\,\ell_P$ & $2.155\cdot10^{-39}$ m \\
IR: $L^* = 4\bar\lambda_e/\xi^{10}$ & $8.698\cdot10^{26}$ m \\
Ratio $L^*/L_0$ & $4.04\cdot10^{65}$ \\
required & $2\pi \approx 6.28$ \\
\midrule
Shortfall & \textbf{65 decades} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{[X] Stage 3 -- a finite window against infinitely many zeros.}
$\ln(L^*/L_0) = 151.06$, that is $16.93$ $\xi$ ladder rungs; the
Berry-Keating count within the window gives ${\sim}1816$ modes. A finite
scale window carries finitely many modes; the zeros are countably
infinite. Even if stages~1 and~2 held, at most an initial segment would
result -- and an initial segment is not a spectral identity.

\emph{Marginal observation, explicitly not booked:} $16.93$ lies close
to 17. Both cutoffs carry their own uncertainties -- the prefactor~4 is
open per R73, $\ell_P$ depends on $G$. Without an error budget this is
not assessable under P35.

\subsection{What the chain achieves}

\textbf{[B] What does carry is a structural statement, and it is not
little:} that
the counting function has the form $(T/2\pi)\ln(T/2\pi)$ at all requires
a \textbf{dilation generator}. A Laplacian on a compact manifold cannot
deliver it (Section~\ref{sec:weyl}). The substantive statement is the
\emph{identification of the structure} -- the unrolled domain carries
the dilation -- not the numerical value $2\pi$.

\section{The missing boundary condition -- test negative}
\label{sec:rand}

$H = xp$ has \emph{continuous} spectrum. The counting function fixes the
mean density, not the individual zeros. What is missing is the boundary
condition that turns the continuum into a discrete spectrum.

\textbf{The natural candidate.} The $\xi$ ladder generates a discrete
subgroup $\Gamma_\xi = \{\xi^n\} < \mathbb{R}_+^*$; the quotient
$\mathbb{R}_+^*/\Gamma_\xi$ is a \emph{circle} of circumference
$L = \ln(1/\xi) = 8.9227$. Admissible dilation frequencies would then be
multiples of $2\pi/L = 0.7042$.

\subsection{Test discipline}

\textbf{[K]} Proximity arguments require three controls (P35): a null
distribution over random periods in the \emph{same} range; artefact
control, because periods larger than the data range automatically
produce small scores; and an out-of-sample check with look-elsewhere
correction.

The artefact control is mandatory: periods above the data range
automatically produce low scores, because all quotients round to the
same integer. At period $2\pi/|\ln(74/75)| = 468$, for instance, all
zeros lie below $102$; a score near zero means nothing there. The null
distribution must cover the same range of periods as the candidates,
or spurious signals arise.

mean $0.2502$):

\begin{keyresult}[Boundary condition]
\textbf{[X]} The $\xi$ ladder does not supply the missing boundary
condition. No $\xi$-derived period candidate survives the out-of-sample
check.
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Why this was structurally to be expected}

\textbf{[K]} A single-period compactification produces an
\emph{equidistant} lattice. The zeros, however, show GUE statistics with
level repulsion (Montgomery-Odlyzko~\cite{montgomery1973,odlyzko1987}): mean normalised spacing $0.9815$
(should be ${\sim}1$), fraction of spacings $< 0.3$ equal to
\textbf{$0.000$} -- against ${\sim}0.04$ for GUE and ${\sim}0.26$ for a
lattice-like spectrum. A circle cannot deliver GUE in principle.

The missing boundary condition is carried by arithmetic (the primes),
not by geometry.

\section{The harmonic approach: $\xi$ as a Tonnetz point}
\label{sec:tonnetz}

The preceding sections treat $\xi$ as a \emph{magnitude} -- a parameter
whose smallness decides its effect. A second approach asks instead about
its \emph{harmonic structure}, that is, about the position of $\xi$ in
the prime lattice. It leads to a different point of contact with the
theory and yields a sharper exclusion.

\subsection{The starting observation}

\textbf{[K]} The prime factorisation
\[
  \frac{1}{\xi} \;=\; 7500 \;=\; 2^2\cdot 3\cdot 5^4
\]
places $\xi$ as a point in Euler's Tonnetz with coordinates
$(a_2, a_3, a_5) = (-2, -1, -4)$. This is the same lattice structure as
in the control case of the musical spiral: the primes 2, 3, 5 as axes of
the 5-limit space. Equivalently in logarithmic form:
\[
  \ln(1/\xi) \;=\; 2\ln 2 + \ln 3 + 4\ln 5 \;=\; 8.922658\ldots
\]

The harmonic reading suggests a different point of contact than the
Laplace operator. A Tonnetz carries three incommensurable logarithmic
frequencies $\ln 2$, $\ln 3$, $\ln 5$; three independent frequencies
generate a quasi-periodic, precisely not equidistant spectrum. The
approach therefore touches the arithmetic of the $\zeta$ function
directly.

\subsection{The responsible point of contact: Weil's formula}

\textbf{[Q]} Weil's explicit formula couples zeros and primes:
\[
  \sum_\rho h(\gamma) \;=\; (\text{archimedean term})
  \;-\; 2\sum_{p,k} \frac{\ln p}{p^{k/2}}\;\hat h(k\ln p).
\]
The dual \emph{length spectrum} of the zeros is therefore
\[
  \mathcal{L} \;=\; \{\,k\ln p \;:\; p \text{ prime},\; k = 1,2,3,\ldots\,\},
\]
that is, multiples of logarithms of \textbf{single} primes. Whether
$\xi$ appears there is directly testable: the Fourier transform of the
zero density must show peaks at the lengths in $\mathcal{L}$.

\subsection{Measurement}

\textbf{[K]} Tested is $F(u) = \bigl|\sum_n e^{i\gamma_n u}\bigr|/N$ for
the first 100 zeros; the background is 3000 random $u$ in the same range
(median $0.0304$, 95\,\% threshold $0.2005$, 99\,\% threshold

\textbf{Resolution limit.} The measurement becomes assessable only once
$u$ spreads the phases over the full circle, that is
$u \gg 2\pi/(\gamma_{\max}-\gamma_{\min}) = 0.02824$. For $u\to 0$ all
phases coincide and $F(u)\to 1$, independently of any structure.
Candidates below this limit -- for instance $\ln(75/74) = 0.01342$ --
are \emph{not measurable} with 100 zeros; they yield neither
confirmation nor refutation.

\subsection{The structural reason}

\begin{keyresult}[Combination against factorisation]
Tonnetz and Euler product share the prime axes but use them in
\textbf{opposite} ways. The Euler product
$\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ logarithmises to a sum over prime
powers: each prime stands on its own, without cross terms -- hence
$\mathcal{L}$ contains only $k\ln p$. The Tonnetz lives on the
opposite: an interval is $2^a 3^b 5^c$ with several non-zero exponents,
and it is precisely the mixing that generates the commas. $\xi$ is such
a mixed point.
\end{keyresult}

\textbf{[X]} $\ln(1/\xi)$ is therefore a \emph{sum} of lengths and not a
length; sums do not appear as peaks in the trace formula. The nearest
genuine length is $3\ln 19 = 8.83332$ at distance $0.08934$ -- without
significance, since proximity within the dense length spectrum says
nothing.

\textbf{Sharpness of the exclusion.} It is structural, not a matter of
magnitude: it holds unchanged if $\xi$ were of order 1. What matters is
not the value of $\xi$ but that $1/\xi$ is composite. This section
therefore carries a stronger reason than any estimate based on the
smallness of $\xi$.

\textbf{Remaining test surface.} The \emph{individual} axes $\ln 2$,
$\ln 3$, $\ln 5$ are lengths in the Weil spectrum -- but they are so for
any theory containing primes at all; the factorisation of 7500 selects
nothing. A statement would arise only if the \emph{exponents} $(2,1,4)$
produced a weighting within the spectrum. The following subsection
tests this.

\subsection{Does the 5-limit include the higher ones?}
\label{sec:einschluss}

The limit extension of Test~B raises the converse question: can it be
shown that the 5-limit already contains the higher limits? The answer
comes in five gradations, and the gradation repeats the pattern of the
whole document (\texttt{z9\_limit\_einschluss.py}).

\textbf{[K] (1) Exactly: no, provably.} $\ln 7 = a\ln2 + b\ln3 +
c\ln5$ with rational $a,b,c$ would mean, after clearing denominators,
$7^q = 2^A 3^B 5^C$ -- contradicting unique factorisation. This holds
for every prime above the limit, hence for infinitely many.

\textbf{[K] (2) As a dense approximation: yes, arbitrarily well.} Since
$\ln2, \ln3, \ln5$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$, the
lattice lies dense in $\mathbb{R}$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrlr}
\toprule

\midrule

$4$ & $7.97\cdot10^{-3}$ & $13.80$ & $(-1,-2,3)$ & $4.4\cdot10^{-4}$ \\
$6$ & $4.45\cdot10^{-3}$ & $7.71$ & $(-5,2,2)$ & $1.4\cdot10^{-4}$ \\

The error of the best approximation $(1,7,-4)$ is exactly
$\ln(4375/4374)$ -- \textbf{the ragisma}. The coarse approximation

comma}. Both are known from the Euler control case: there as

approximation to the 7-axis. It is the same quantity read from the
opposite direction.

\textbf{[K] (3) At finite resolution: yes, indistinguishable.} The peak
width of the measurement in Section~\ref{sec:bikoh} is
$2\pi/(\gamma_{\max}-\gamma_{\min}) = 0.0021$; the ragisma, at
$2.29\cdot10^{-4}$, is only $0.11$ peak widths:

The fine approximation is indistinguishable from $\ln 7$, the coarse

statement about the measuring procedure, not about the arithmetic.

\textbf{[K] (4) Amplitude-weighted: no, and clearly so.} The Tenney
weight of the approximation $(1,7,-4)$ is $3.7\cdot10^{-7}$ against
$1/7$ for $\ln 7$ as an axis of its own -- a factor $3.9\cdot10^{5}$.
This also explains \emph{why} the approximation can be so accurate: it
requires large exponents, and large exponents mean small weight.
Accuracy and weight run opposite to one another.

\textbf{[B] (5) By tempering: yes, but by stipulation.} Declaring the
ragisma equal to $1$ makes $7 = 2\cdot 3^7/5^4$ exact in the tempered
system; likewise, tempering out Archytas' comma makes the
approximation $64/9$ exact.

\begin{keyresult}[Inclusion does not arise from structure]
The 5-limit does not include the higher limits -- unless approximation,
limited resolution or tempering is allowed to count as inclusion. This
is the same pattern as everywhere in this document: inclusion arises
through \emph{rationalisation} (tempering) or through \emph{blur}
(resolution), never through structure. Likewise Euler's spiral closes
only by tempering, and likewise coupling in the Weil spectrum sits only
at exact prime powers (Section~\ref{sec:bikoh}).
\end{keyresult}

For $\xi$ nothing new follows, but a confirmation: that $\xi$ is a pure
5-limit point cannot be cured by any extension -- and the
approximations that might replace an extension carry no weight.

\subsection{Measurement instead of assertion: the bicoherence test}
\label{sec:bikoh}

The statement that the Euler product has no cross terms was up to this
point argued from the \emph{form} of the product. It can be measured --
with the procedure responsible for it and with built-in controls.

\textbf{Setup.} The sequence of zeros is taken as a point measure; its
transform in the length variable is $F(u) = \sum_n e^{i\gamma_n u}$.
Averaged over height blocks, bispectrum and bicoherence~\cite{kim1979} follow as in
Section~\ref{sec:quellen}. The zeros are computed here via the
Riemann-Siegel formula: 2469 of them up to $t = 3000$ (Riemann-von
Mangoldt expects $2468.6$), residual error $7.5\cdot10^{-3}$ in
$\gamma$, corresponding to $0.02$~rad of phase error at
$u \approx 2.6$.

\textbf{Test plan.} The Weil spectrum contains $k\ln p$. This yields
predictions with positive \emph{and} negative controls: if the sum of
two lengths is again a length -- that is, a prime power -- coupling must
appear; if it leads to a composite number, none may appear.

\textbf{Pre-check.} The power of $F(u)$ shows the individual lengths
$\ln 2 \ldots \ln 13$ all above the 99\,\% threshold. The background
must be cleaned of the 13 genuine lengths in the window -- random $u$
hit one of them with about 2\,\% probability and otherwise form the
outlier tail (median $0.055$ against a $99\,\%$ threshold of $0.881$
after cleaning).

from 2000 phase-randomised realisations per pair):

\section{Averaging, tempering, resolution: where does the instrument sit?}
\label{sec:instrument}

Euler's spiral closes only by tempering (Section~\ref{sec:faktor}), and
the 5-limit contains the higher limits only by approximation,

closure arises not from the structure but from something added from
outside. It is worth determining what that addition is -- for in three
domains it sits in three different places.

\subsection{Three domains, three locations}

\textbf{For hearing}, the averaging sits in the \emph{receiver}:
critical bands, finite frequency resolution, fusion of closely spaced
tones. The measuring instrument becomes part of the result. Tempering
here is not an arbitrary stipulation but follows from a property of the
listener.

\textbf{In pure mathematics} there is no receiver. The ratios exist
regardless of whether anyone distinguishes them. If something is to
close that does not close of itself, tempering must be inserted as a
\emph{stipulation} -- a deliberate intervention, documented in the
comma that thereby disappears.

\textbf{In nature} it is open whether there is a receiver. The question
therefore arises whether the fractal correction plays that role.

\subsection{The testable form}

\emph{comma}: a ratio of smooth numbers, that is, of products of small
primes. The corpus knows one residue -- the $\approx 7$~eV of P-315-2,
$45{,}000$ times the measurement floor. The question is thus testable:
does this residue have comma structure?

\textbf{[K] It does not.} The best rational approximations to

7-limit smooth.

\emph{On test conduct:} the smoothness test must be coupled to a
condition on approximation quality. Otherwise the trivial
approximation $1/1$ counts as smooth although it misses the target
value by 100\,\%.

\textbf{[B]} A further clarification is easily overlooked: in FFGFT the

\section{Sources and methodological placement}
\label{sec:quellen}

The procedures used in this document are standard, and so are their
pitfalls. This section places the usable literature and states where
the reach of the procedures employed is bounded.

\subsection{Load-bearing sources}

\textbf{Berry \& Keating (1999)}~\cite{berry1999}. The basis of
Section~\ref{sec:zelle}. Comparison of the counting functions suggests
that the $t_n$ are eigenvalues of an unknown Hermitian operator
obtained by quantising a classical system with Hamiltonian
$H_{cl} = xp$. The work also states the requirement on such a system:
the \enquote{Riemann dynamics} would have to be chaotic and possess
periodic orbits \emph{whose periods are multiples of logarithms of the
primes}. That is precisely the length spectrum of
Section~\ref{sec:tonnetz} -- and the condition on which the Tonnetz
fails, since it yields sums rather than multiples.

\textbf{Sierra (2019)}~\cite{sierra2019} \textbf{and Sierra \& Townsend
(2008)}~\cite{sierra2008}. These show the state of the Hilbert-P\'olya programme:
spectral realisations via $xp$ with boundary interaction, via Landau
levels, and most recently via a massless Dirac fermion in Rindler
spacetime with delta potentials on the square-free integers. Notable
for the present question: all these constructions encode the arithmetic
\emph{explicitly} (square-free numbers, modular forms, boundary
functions from the Riemann-Siegel formula). None derives it from a
geometry. This supports the finding of Section~\ref{sec:weyl} from the
other side: where the zeros are realised spectrally, it happens by
building in number theory, not by choosing a space.

\textbf{P\'apics (2012)}~\cite{papics2012}. The work
methodologically closest to Section~\ref{sec:gewichtung}. It examines
whether detected frequencies stand in harmonic relation to one
another -- the same problem in asteroseismology. Central result:
combination frequencies occur in random data with high probability, and
as data quality rises the distinction between genuine and spurious
combinations becomes \emph{harder}, not easier. The usable
discriminator is not the existence of individual combinations but the
\emph{number} of lowest-order combinations compared against simulations
with matching properties. Exactly this approach -- comparison against a
simulated null distribution rather than evaluation of individual hits
-- underlies the permutation tests of this document.

\textbf{P\'ol\'oskei et al.\ (2018)}~\cite{poloskei2018}. Treats the
procedure actually responsible for the question of harmonic coupling.
Bicoherence
\[
  b^2(f_1,f_2) = \frac{|B(f_1,f_2)|^2}
  {A\{|X(f_1)X(f_2)|^2\}\,A\{|X(f_1{+}f_2)|^2\}}
\]
measures not amplitudes but \emph{phase coupling}: whether
$f_3 = f_1 + f_2$ occurs with a fixed phase relation
$\varphi_3 = \varphi_1 + \varphi_2 + \text{const}$. Decisive for the
present use is the warning about false positives: for non-stationary or
short signals, classical bicoherence yields high values \emph{without}
any coupling. The proposed remedy is a Monte Carlo procedure using the
measured Fourier amplitudes with \emph{randomised phases}, from which
the null distribution $\rho(b)$ and a critical value $b_c(\alpha)$ are
obtained. The trade-off is also stated: the number of averages can be
increased only at the cost of frequency resolution
($\Delta f = f_s N/M$).

\subsection{Further usable procedures}

The following methods fall below the Fourier resolution limit by
assuming a model (a sum of exponentials) instead of transforming
blindly. They have not been employed here but would be the next stage:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Subspace methods:} MUSIC (Schmidt), ESPRIT
    (Roy/Kailath), Min-Norm. They decompose the covariance matrix into
    signal and noise subspaces.
  \item \textbf{Matrix pencil}~\cite{hua1990} for damped and undamped sinusoids.
  \item \textbf{Iterative methods:} RELAX, NOMP; and Volterra-series
    approaches for the self-consistent removal of combination
    frequencies (Lares-Martiz et al., MNRAS 2020).
  \item \textbf{Multitaper} (Thomson) with an F-test for line
    components; \textbf{multichannel singular spectrum analysis} for
    extracting dynamical modes without a Fourier basis.
\end{itemize}

On the magnitude of the gain: in a published comparison of three
frequencies at 440/445/450~Hz over 100~ms, ESPRIT attains a frequency
RMSE of $0.087$~Hz against $2.88$~Hz for spectral MUSIC -- the
difference between model classes thus exceeds the difference from
Fourier analysis.

\subsection{Reach of the procedures employed}

\textbf{[B]} The scripts \texttt{z6} and \texttt{z7} use the bare
Fourier sum $F(u) = |\sum_n e^{i\gamma_n u}|/N$ -- the simplest tool
available. Three limitations follow:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{No coupling measurement.} $F(u)$ measures whether
    structure sits at length $u$ at all, not whether two lengths are
    \emph{coupled}. The structural claim that the Euler product has no
    cross terms is argued in this document from the form of the
    product and measured in Section~\ref{sec:bikoh}; the remaining

  \item \emph{Statistics.} Sections \ref{sec:tonnetz} and
    \ref{sec:gewichtung} compute with 100 zeros from the literature;
    for bispectra that is too few. Section \ref{sec:bikoh} therefore
    computes 2469 zeros itself.
  \item \emph{Not a time signal.} The sequence of zeros is not a
    measured signal; stationarity is undefined, and the standard tests
    for non-stationarity do not apply directly.
\end{enumerate}

These limitations do not touch the \textbf{[X]} findings of this
document: the Weyl obstruction (Section~\ref{sec:weyl}) and the

structural arguments and independent of the choice of spectral method.
They concern only the numerical tests, whose force is thereby bounded

\section{Closing reflection: why one cannot compute backwards}

At the end stands an image that draws the individual findings together,
and an interpretation that goes beyond them. The two must be kept
apart.

\subsection{What the calculations show}

The primes are axes -- in the Tonnetz as in the Euler product. The same
numbers, the same lattice structure. For simple ratios the inference
back to the underlying harmony is unambiguous: $\ln 2$, $\ln 3$,
$\ln 5$ appear as clear lines in the spectrum of the zeros, each in its
place, each assignable.

With composition this unambiguity is lost, for two reasons, both
visible in the tests.

The first is \emph{ambiguity}. A simple ratio has few possible
decompositions. A composite one has many -- and the further the lattice
is spanned, the denser the possibilities lie, until in the end every
number fits somehow. It is not the rarity of a combination that creates
the difficulty but its multiplicity of readings: where everything is
reachable, reaching says nothing.

The second reason weighs heavier. The Euler product treats each prime
\emph{on its own}, without cross terms; its spectrum knows only
$k\ln p$, multiples of individual prime logarithms. As soon as a ratio
mixes several primes it is no longer a single harmony but a
superposition -- and superpositions do not appear in the spectrum as
lines of their own. $\xi = 2^2/(2^4\cdot3\cdot5^4)$ is such a
superposition.

An image from practice captures it exactly: from the sound of a single
reed one can infer the fundamental. In a full chord across several
registers one hears the mixture -- but the fundamental of the mixture
does not exist. There are only the fundamentals of the individual
reeds. $\xi$ is a chord, not a note.

\subsection{The direction that works -- and the one that does not}

This allows a precise statement of what is actually closed here. It is
not the connection between harmony and structure; it is its
\emph{reversibility}.

Forwards it carries: from simple ratios the composite can be built,
step by step, each step traceable. This is how intervals arise from
fifths and thirds, and how $\xi$ arises from $2$, $3$ and $5$.

Backwards it does not carry. Composition is not invertible because it
destroys information: many paths lead to the same place, and the path
taken can no longer be read off the result. This is not a weakness of
method that better techniques could remedy, but a property of the
matter itself. The tests of this document confirmed it at three
different points: at the weighting strength, at the limit extension,
and at the rigidity of the Weil weights.

\subsection{Interpretation}

\begin{highlight}[Interpretation, not a finding]
It is natural to suppose that complex formations in nature rest on such
harmonies -- that the composite is built from simple ratios, as a chord
from notes. This conjecture is old and has been fruitful in many
respects; it also stands behind the construction of $\xi$.

What this document shows is something else and more modest: even if
that is so, the path cannot be walked backwards. Reconstructing the
underlying harmonies from the finished formation is not difficult but
impossible -- composition has erased the assignment.
\end{highlight}

The first sentence is an interpretation and remains one; nothing in
this document supports it and nothing refutes it. The second is
computed.

Between the two lies the actual yield: whoever suspects a harmonic
order behind complex structures must establish it \emph{forwards} --
by construction and prediction. The way back, inferring the order from
the finished structure, is not open. This holds for the Riemann
hypothesis, and it holds more generally.

\section{The Fourier transform as a projection -- and why that settles it}
\label{sec:projektion}

This document has applied the Fourier transform to 100 and 2469 zeros
respectively (Sections~\ref{sec:tonnetz} and~\ref{sec:bikoh}) and made
prime lengths visible in the process. It is worth understanding that
operation precisely -- for it reveals why neither classical FFT, nor
quantum Fourier transform, nor a photonic system can solve the Riemann
hypothesis.

\subsection{What a Fourier transform does and does not do}

\textbf{[K]} A Fourier transform -- classical, optical, or as a quantum
Fourier transform (QFT) -- is a \emph{projection}: it translates one
representation into another without generating new information. What is not
contained in the input data does not appear in the output.

This is visible concretely in the measurements of this document. The
Fourier transform of the zeros shows peaks at $\ln 2$, $\ln 3$, $\ln 5$,
$\ln 7$, $\ln 11$, $\ln 13$ -- because this information is \emph{already
contained in the zeros}, via the Euler product. It shows no peak at
$\ln(1/\xi) = 2\ln2+\ln3+4\ln5$ -- because that information is not there.
The transform \emph{invents} no structure; it \emph{projects} the existing
structure onto a different coordinate system.

\textbf{Consequence:} A Fourier analysis of the zeros cannot solve the
Riemann hypothesis, because it does not generate the arithmetic
information -- it can only find it if it is already there. And the decisive
information (exactly where the zeros lie) must be obtained by computation,
not by projection.

\subsection{The structure of Shor's algorithm}

\textbf{[K]} Shor's algorithm decomposes factorisation into two steps.
The first -- evaluating $f(x) = a^x \bmod N$ -- is classical overhead that
cannot be parallelised; it grows with bit size. The second -- the QFT for
period finding -- is the actual quantum operation.

But the QFT too \emph{generates} no new information: it finds the period
$r$ already present in the prepared states. \emph{Obtaining} those states
is the limiting step. The same holds for the zeros: the Fourier analysis
finds what the Riemann-Siegel formula has already computed. The QFT does
not solve the RH because it does not compute the zeros -- it takes them as
given.

\subsection{The topological obstruction}

\textbf{[K]} Section~\ref{sec:weyl} shows the decisive obstruction: the
density of zeros grows like $T\ln T$, while every compact manifold (and
every finite qubit register) has eigenvalue density $N(\lambda) \sim
C\lambda^{d/2}$. That is not a power -- and hence structurally incompatible.
A quantum computer with $n$ qubits spans a compact, finite state space; it
cannot carry the $T\ln T$ density as an eigenvalue spectrum. This is not a
technical but a structural obstacle.

\textbf{The connection to Shor:} Shor's algorithm factorises concrete
numbers efficiently on quantum hardware -- a real achievement for a specific
problem. But a general exponential advantage of quantum computers over
classical methods is not established, and for the RH the Weyl obstruction
applies without qualification.

\subsection{Projection and counting}

The Fourier transform operates in the additive space of logarithms; the
Riemann hypothesis is a statement about the multiplicative structure of the
Euler product. A projection translates the representation -- it does not
change the question.

\begin{highlight}[The core of the separation]
The Fourier transform is a projection of multiplicative structure onto an
additive basis. It can \emph{find} the prime structure when it is present in
the input data -- as the bicoherence test demonstrates. It cannot
\emph{generate} it. The Riemann hypothesis is a statement about the
consistency of factorisation -- and that question cannot be answered by any
projection, whether classical, optical, or as a quantum Fourier transform.
\end{highlight}

\textbf{Note on the Shor documents in the corpus.} Older documents
(Docs.~075, 076, 147, 176) draw a connection between FFGFT and Shor's
algorithm. That connection is associative, not substantive: factorising a
particular number is structurally distinct from a statement about all
primes. Those documents were revised in August 2026 accordingly (R75,
Doc.~190).

\section{Relaxation as a third mechanism}
\label{sec:relaxation}

The preceding sections treat procedures that \emph{transform}: Fourier
transform, quantum Fourier transform, optical interference. All are
projections and generate no information (Section~\ref{sec:projektion}).
There is a third mechanism that does not transform but \emph{settles}:
physical relaxation into an energy minimum.

\subsection{The mechanism}

\textbf{[Q]} An Ising system minimises
\begin{equation}
  H_{\text{Ising}} = -\tfrac12 \sum_{ij} J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j
  - \sum_i h_i \sigma_i ,
\end{equation}
and every combinatorial optimisation problem maps polynomially onto this
form. Whoever finds the ground state has solved the problem. Analog Ising
machines -- for instance coherent optical setups of degenerate parametric
oscillators -- realise this as physical relaxation rather than algorithmic
iteration. The corpus treats this approach in Doc.~161 (T0 Ising machine)
and Doc.~162 (comparison of optimisation frameworks).

\textbf{The method is established.} Programmable setups with 100 spins and
all-to-all coupling, as well as networks with 2000 nodes, are published
(McMahon et al., \emph{Science} 354, 2016; Inagaki et al., \emph{Science}
354, 2016).

\subsection{Noise as a tool}

\textbf{[Q]} The central difficulty of analog Ising machines is getting
trapped in local minima. Noise resolves this: it forces spin flips even
where they are energetically unfavourable, opening a wider region of phase
space. This is not conjecture but measured practice with a quantitative
law.

Decisive is an \emph{optimal} noise strength: too little leaves the system
trapped, too much drives it into a Boltzmann sampling regime in which the
ground state is likewise missed. The optimal strength follows a scaling law
\begin{equation}
  \gamma \sim \beta \sqrt{C},
\end{equation}
with coupling strength $\beta$ and mean number $C$ of connections per spin;
the fit reaches $R^2 = 0.97$ for small problems (Mys, Verschaffelt \& Van
der Sande, arXiv:2508.19107, 2025). This allows the quality to be predicted
without a parameter scan -- the times to solution obtained lie at about
twice the optimum.

\subsection{What relaxation achieves and what it does not}

\textbf{[K]} Relaxation is \emph{not} a projection. It does not transform
between representations but settles into a state. The exclusion of
Section~\ref{sec:projektion} therefore does not apply directly; the
mechanism must be examined in its own right.

\textbf{[X]} It fails at the same point, but for a different reason. The
potential $J_{ij}$ must \emph{already} carry the sought structure: the
optimisation problem is encoded into the coupling matrix, and relaxation
finds what is written there. An arithmetic structure not contained in
$J_{ij}$ arises no more from settling than from transforming.

There is also a practical limit that the sources themselves state: not all
problems reach high success rates. In the study cited, five of the forty
Gset instances yield, for \emph{none} of the models examined, a value
within $99.5\,\%$ of the best known cut. Analog Ising machines are capable
heuristics, not exact solvers -- the ground-state problem remains NP-hard.

\begin{keyresult}[Three mechanisms, one limit]
Transformation (Fourier, QFT, optics) projects and generates no
information. Relaxation settles and finds what is in the potential. Both
presuppose that the sought structure is already present -- in the input
data or in the coupling matrix respectively. The Riemann hypothesis is a
statement about that structure itself.
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{The question, properly posed}

This allows the initial question to be put more sharply. \enquote{Can a
quantum computer solve the Riemann hypothesis?} confuses the tool with the
object -- and forces every technology to be examined separately.

The tenable formulation reads: \emph{Is the Riemann hypothesis a question
that can be answered by transformation or relaxation?} The answer is no,
and it holds independently of technology: both mechanisms presuppose the
arithmetic structure rather than generating it. The limit lies not in the
tool but in the object.

\section{Settled items}

The questions raised in the course of this document are all decided.
They are recorded here so that they are not taken up again.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Z-1 -- weighting by the exponents $(2,1,4)$:} decided
    in the negative. Neither a weighting strength nor a limit
    extension produces selection; above all, the weights of the
    explicit formula are not free parameters
    (Section~\ref{sec:gewichtung}).
  \item \textbf{Z-2 -- meaning of the lattice factor $(1-4^{1-s})$:}
    clarified. The factor is the Dirichlet signature of Jacobi's
    four-square theorem, $r_4(n) = 8\sum_{d\mid n,\;4\nmid d} d$, and
    encodes the divisibility condition \enquote{$4$ does not divide
    $d$}. The associated ladder of zeros $s = 1 + 2\pi i k/\ln 4$ with
    spacing $\pi/\ln 2 = 4.5324$ is a property of the sum of four
    squares; the nearest quantity of the framework (the prefactor 4
    from $L^{*}$, R73) is $12\,\%$ away. The $4$ is indeed the number
    of squares and hence the dimension, but it acts arithmetically
    here, not geometrically
    (\texttt{z12\_gitterfaktor\_waermekern.py}).
  \item \textbf{Z-3 -- heat kernel and theta-series fine structure:}
    void. The difference between D4 and $\mathbb{Z}^4$ rests on a
    parity condition -- D4 occupies only even norms, and at those the
    two lattices agree exactly. No test surface follows: at small $t$
    the Weyl term dominates both series equally, at large $t$ both are
    exponentially small, and the informative location is the Casimir
    point $s = -1/2$, whose evaluation is given in Doc.~314.
  \item \textbf{Z-4 -- bicoherence test:} carried out and confirming
    in six of six controls (Section~\ref{sec:bikoh}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Not recommended:} further search for $\xi$ periods in the
zeros. Section~\ref{sec:rand} excludes this structurally via the GUE
argument, not merely numerically.
