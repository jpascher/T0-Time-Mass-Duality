\section{Preparation and Transformation: Two Separate Processes}

\subsection*{Summary}

Shor's algorithm falls into two processes that are regularly conflated
although they have nothing to do with each other:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Preparation} & \textbf{Transformation} \\
\midrule
Input & numbers $a$, $N$ & prepared signal \\
Output & signal with $f(x) = a^x \bmod N$ & frequency representation \\
Nature & classical arithmetic & wave operation \\
Effort & \textbf{expensive} ($O(n^3)$ gates) & \textbf{cheap} ($O(n^2)$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Preparation is difficult because modular exponentiation is difficult --
equally so in every technology. It does \emph{not} depend on
quantum-mechanical properties. The transformation is the same wave
operation in every technology and cheap in each. The difference between
optics and a qubit register lies in neither, but in operating
temperature and coupling mechanism.

\begin{keyresult}[Three separate parameter spaces]
To avoid confusion this document distinguishes strictly:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Geometric space:} $\xi = 4/30000 \approx 1.333\times10^{-4}$ --
    the structural parameter of FFGFT. Fixed, not adjustable.
  \item \textbf{Algebraic space:} $\xi_{\text{Higgs}} \approx
    1.038\times10^{-5}$ -- field correction in the vacuum expectation value.
  \item \textbf{Number-theoretic space} $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$: \emph{no}
    universal parameter. The period $r$ is determined by
    $\mathrm{lcm}(p-1,q-1)$ -- an individual arithmetic property of $N$.
\end{enumerate}
Factorisation lives exclusively in the third space. Neither $\xi$ nor
$\xi_{\text{Higgs}}$ has a function there.
\end{keyresult}

\section{Preparation}
\label{sec:aufbereitung}

\textbf{Input:} the numbers $a$ and $N$.
\textbf{Output:} a state or signal carrying the sequence
$f(x) = a^x \bmod N$.

\subsection{What happens here}

\textbf{[K]} Modular exponentiation is a \emph{classical,
deterministic} function. Its evaluation contains no quantum-mechanical
component: on a quantum computer it is executed as a reversible
classical circuit, in an optical setup it is imprinted as a spatial or
spectral structure.

\subsection{The cost}

\textbf{[K]} The effort depends on the arithmetic, not on the chosen
technology:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Realisation & Effort & for RSA-2048 \\
\midrule
Quantum circuit & $O(n^3)$ gates & $8.6\cdot10^{9}$ gates \\
Optical mask & $2^n$ elements & $10^{617}$ elements \\
Classical table & $2^n$ values & $10^{617}$ values \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The quantum circuit is the cheapest known variant: it encodes the
computational rule rather than the values and therefore requires only
polynomial effort. An optical mask would have to carry every function
value individually -- for RSA-2048 more elements than the observable
universe has atoms ($10^{80}$).

\begin{keyresult}[Preparation is an arithmetic problem]
It is expensive because modular exponentiation is expensive -- and that
holds in every technology. A method promising to bypass it shifts the
effort rather than removing it. \textbf{Preparation does not depend on
quantum-mechanical properties.}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{What superposition contributes here}

\textbf{[X]} Nothing. The state $\sum_x \ket{x}\ket{a^x \bmod N}$ does
contain all pairs, but a measurement yields exactly one -- drawn at
random. Afterwards one knows as much as after a single classical
evaluation. Superposition does not simplify the arithmetic; it applies
the same circuit across all inputs.


\section{The Hybrid Preparation: Binary Control Meets Analogue Wave}
\label{sec:hybrid}

Pure binary implementation of modular exponentiation drowns in $O(n^3)$
gates. Purely analogue implementation fails at thermal noise and the
modulo problem. A hybrid approach exploits the strengths of both:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The binary part (the control):} Exact, noise-free
    discretisation of the modulo logic -- governs which phase paths are
    enabled.
  \item \textbf{The analogue part (the wave):} Continuous, parallel
    phase accumulation via interference and mixer structures in real time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What happens physically}

The analogue wave performs multiplication via \emph{coherent phase
superposition}. Mixing two waves with phases $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$
produces:
\[
  \Psi_{\text{total}} \propto e^{i\phi_1} + e^{i\phi_2}
\]
or for coupled systems $\ket{\phi_1} \otimes \ket{\phi_2}$. In classical
engineering this is coherent summation or frequency mixing; in quantum
physics the same mathematical structure is called superposition.

\subsection{Why physics introduces new terms here}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Discretisation by single quanta:} At the single-photon
    level the analogue wave becomes a probability distribution --
    analogue superposition becomes quantum superposition.
  \item \textbf{Escaping the noise problem:} Classical analogue mixers
    suffer from thermal noise ($k \cdot T \cdot B$). Operating in the
    single-photon regime enables noise-free switching via quantum error
    correction.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusion on hybrid preparation}

\begin{keyresult}[Hybrid preparation = binary-controlled interference network]
The hybrid preparation is a \emph{phased-array-type interference
network} that is binary-conditioned. The ``superposition'' here is the
physical description of analogue wave superposition -- not a quantum-
mechanical magic.
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Cost of hybrid preparation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Implementation & Gate count & Characteristic \\
\midrule
Purely binary & $O(n^3)$ & Drowns for large $n$ \\
Purely analogue & --- & Fails at noise \\
Hybrid & Reduced & Phased-array interference \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


\section{The Fourier Transform}
\label{sec:ft}

\textbf{Input:} the prepared signal.
\textbf{Output:} its frequency representation.

\subsection{What happens here}

\textbf{[K]} The Fourier transform is a \emph{wave operation}. It takes
no interest in where the signal comes from or what it means -- it
translates one representation into another. No new information arises:
what is absent from the input signal does not appear in the output.

\subsection{The cost}

\textbf{[K]} It is cheap in every realisation:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Realisation & Effort & Remark \\
\midrule
Quantum Fourier transform & $O(n^2)$ gates & sequential \\
Optical MZI network & transit time & simultaneous, speed of light \\
Classical FFT & $O(2^n \cdot n)$ & on explicit values \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In the quantum register and in the optical network the effort is small.
The difference between the two lies in operating temperature and
coupling mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:photonik}), not in the transform
itself.

\subsection{What it is for}

The period $r$ sought is a \emph{global} property of the sequence: no
single function value carries it; it becomes visible only by comparing
several values. The Fourier transform performs this comparison
physically -- interference cancels everything except the frequencies
$k/r$, and from a few measurements $r$ follows by continued-fraction
expansion.

\begin{keyresult}[Two separate processes]
\emph{Preparation:} arithmetic, expensive, difficult independently of
technology.
\emph{Transformation:} wave-mechanical, cheap, the same operation in
every technology.

The two are unconnected. Preparation can be improved without touching
the transform, and vice versa.
\end{keyresult}

\section{Photonic Realisation}
\label{sec:photonik}

\subsection{MZI networks}

A Mach-Zehnder interferometer realises phase rotations
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi jk}{n},
\end{equation}
which correspond exactly to the twiddle factors of the discrete Fourier
transform. A network of such interferometers performs the Fourier transform as
a direct wave operation.

\textbf{[B]} The Fourier transform is therefore not a distinguishing feature of
the quantum computer. It is a wave operation realisable optically,
acoustically, or electronically. The difference lies in state preparation, not
in the transform.

\subsection{The thermal advantage of optical realisation}

\textbf{[K]} Whether a mode sits in its ground state without cooling is
decided by the ratio $h\nu/(k_B T)$. The mean thermal occupation is
\begin{equation}
  \bar n = \frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_B T} - 1}.
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
System & $\nu$ (Hz) & $T$ (K) & $h\nu/k_BT$ & $\bar n$ \\
\midrule
Superconducting qubit & $5.0\cdot10^{9}$ & $300$ & $8.0\cdot10^{-4}$
  & $1.25\cdot10^{3}$ \\
Superconducting qubit & $5.0\cdot10^{9}$ & $0.020$ & $12.0$
  & $6.2\cdot10^{-6}$ \\
Photon 1550\,nm & $1.93\cdot10^{14}$ & $300$ & $30.9$
  & $3.7\cdot10^{-14}$ \\
Photon 600\,nm & $5.0\cdot10^{14}$ & $300$ & $80.0$
  & $1.8\cdot10^{-35}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $5$~GHz and room temperature some \textbf{1250 thermal excitations} sit
in the mode -- the system is fully thermalised. For an occupation below
$0.01$, $T < 52$~mK is required. At optical frequencies the ground state is
occupied essentially exactly at room temperature.

\textbf{Cooling cost.} The Carnot limit demands at least $3\cdot10^{4}$
units of work per unit of heat removed at $10$~mK; in practice considerably
more. A dilution refrigerator draws $10$--$25$~kW continuously; an optical
network draws none.

\begin{keyresult}[Thermodynamically privileged, not computationally]
The thermal advantage follows directly from $h\nu/k_BT$ and is the
strongest known advantage of photonic over superconducting realisation. It
concerns \emph{infrastructure} -- cooling, energy, size -- not computational
power. Both realisations can in principle run the same algorithms;
photonics has difficulties of its own (deterministic two-photon gates,
photon loss as the dominant error channel).
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Where the exponential dimension comes from}

\textbf{[K]} An opposition of \enquote{optics versus quantum computer}
would be inaccurate -- it would set a technology against a computational
model. Photonic quantum computers exist (KLM scheme, measurement-based
approaches). What matters is solely the structure of the state space:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Arrangement & Dimension \\
\midrule
classical wave in $n$ modes & $n$ \\
\emph{one} photon in $n$ modes & $n$ \quad(the same) \\
$n$ entangled two-level systems & $2^n$ \\
$m$ indistinguishable photons in $n$ modes & $\binom{n+m-1}{m}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A single photon in an MZI network realises the same $n$-dimensional unitary
as a classical wave -- classically simulable. With several indistinguishable
photons the dimension grows exponentially: 25 photons in 50 modes give
$3.5\cdot10^{19}$. The jump comes from the \emph{tensor product structure},
not from the carrier medium.

\subsection{What carries the computational advantage -- an open question}

\textbf{[K]} It would be natural to attribute the advantage to entanglement.
The Gottesman-Knill theorem (1998) refutes this: any circuit consisting
solely of Clifford gates ($H$, $S$, CNOT) is classically simulable in
polynomial time, regardless of the entanglement produced. The GHZ state
$(\ket{0\cdots0} + \ket{1\cdots1})/\sqrt2$ is maximally entangled and arises
from $H$ and $n-1$ CNOTs -- at $1000$ qubits some $2\cdot10^{6}$ bits
suffice for the complete classical description.

Further counterexamples: DQC1 shows a conjectured advantage with almost no
entanglement; boson sampling rests on indistinguishability and permanents.
Jozsa and Linden (2003) show entanglement to be \emph{necessary} for
exponential advantage with pure states -- about sufficiency the theorem says
nothing.

\textbf{[X]} Which quantity carries the computational advantage is not
settled. Contextuality, non-stabiliserness (\enquote{magic}) and the
interference structure itself are discussed; none of these candidates is
established as the sole carrier. This document names the question as open
rather than answering it.

\textbf{What follows for factorisation.} The optical realisation does not
solve the problem: the limiting step is state preparation -- the evaluation
of $a^x \bmod N$ over all $x$ -- and that is arithmetic. A network can
transform a prepared superposition; it cannot create one.

\subsection{Topological constraint}

\textbf{[K]} For a wave in phase space to form a closed resonance, the
\emph{logarithmic winding number} $W = \log_2(r)$ must be rational -- not the
frequency ratio $r$ itself:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Case & Ratio $r$ & Winding number $W$ & Orbit \\
\midrule
Pure fifth & $3/2$ (rational) & $0.584963$ (irrational) & open \\
Tempered fifth & $2^{7/12}$ (irrational) & $7/12$ (rational) & closed \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Undamped oscillations on pure numerical ratios produce irrational winding
numbers and diverge as an open spiral. An optical network therefore requires a
discrete, rationalised phase grid to achieve sharp resonance points -- closure
is an artefact of discretisation.

\section{Structural Limits}

\subsection{The Weyl obstruction}

\textbf{[K]} A quantum computer with $n$ qubits spans a compact, finite state
space. By Weyl's law its eigenvalue density grows by a power law:
\begin{equation}
  N(\lambda) \sim C_d \cdot \lambda^{d/2}.
\end{equation}
Arithmetic counting densities, by contrast, grow as
\begin{equation}
  N(T) \sim T\ln T
\end{equation}
(Riemann-von Mangoldt). $T\ln T$ is not a power.

\textbf{Consequence:} a finite state space cannot in principle carry such a
spectrum in full -- it carries at most an initial segment. Infinite spectra of
this density would require an \emph{unrolled dilation domain} (Docs.~315, 316),
not a compact resonance space.

\begin{keyresult}[Important delimitation]
This obstruction does \textbf{not} apply to Shor's algorithm. Shor seeks
\emph{one} period of \emph{one concrete} number $N$ -- a finite task for which
a register of $2n+3$ qubits suffices. The Weyl obstruction concerns the
complete representation of \emph{infinite} arithmetic spectra. Invoking it
against Shor overextends it.

What actually limits Shor at large $N$ is the gate count of modular
exponentiation (growing as $n^3$) and the error-correction overhead (roughly
$10^3$ physical per logical qubit). For RSA-2048: about $4099$ logical qubits,
$8.6\times10^9$ gates, $4.1\times10^6$ physical qubits. That is a
\emph{resource limit} -- in principle surmountable by better hardware, unlike
a structural one.
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Primes as generators}

\textbf{[K]} The load-bearing resonance structure rests on primes. The
bicoherence test on 2469 Riemann zeros (Doc.~316) shows phase coupling exactly
at prime powers ($b^2 = 0.76$--$0.85$) and none at composite numbers
($b^2 = 0.018$--$0.025$).

The physical counterpart is the harmonic series: in $n f_0$, exactly the prime
orders introduce new tonal qualities; $4 = 2\cdot2$, $6 = 2\cdot3$,
$9 = 3\cdot3$ are combinations of axes already present.

\section{Placing the Quantum Computer}

\textbf{[K]} Shor's algorithm factorises on quantum hardware in theoretically
$O((\log N)^3)$ -- a real achievement for a specific problem. It does not
follow that:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{[X]} \emph{Quantum computers hold a general exponential
    advantage.} This is not established for the vast majority of problems.
    Shor is a single case, not evidence of a general advantage.
  \item \textbf{[X]} \emph{The advantage lies in the Fourier transform
    itself.} That is a wave operation and optically realisable. The bottleneck
    is state preparation.
  \item \textbf{[X]} \emph{Quantum computers contribute to structural
    questions of number theory.} The Weyl obstruction applies to finite state
    spaces without qualification.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The core:} reading out a period is a spectroscopic measurement problem
on prepared wave fields. The measurement finds what was prepared; the
preparation is the expensive part.

\textbf{The load-bearing sentence.} The Fourier transform is a projection -- it
finds structure but does not generate it. This holds equally for the classical
FFT, the optical realisation, and the QFT. Factorisation lives in the space
$\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$, where the geometry parameters of FFGFT have no
function.
