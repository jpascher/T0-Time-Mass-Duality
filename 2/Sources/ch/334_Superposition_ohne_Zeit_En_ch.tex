\section*{Preliminary note}

This document builds on Doc.~307 (time in the state space or beside
it) and Doc.~333 (superposition in FFGFT), without repeating their
content. The specific focus here: what does it mean concretely for
the interpretation of \emph{superposition, entanglement and collapse}
that standard QM has no time operator? And what changes structurally
when $\tilde{T}\cdot m=1$ gives every mode its own time scale?

% ============================================================
\section{The Classical Background — Instantaneity as an Inheritance}

\subsection{Classical mechanics: time runs, calculations are instantaneous}

Instantaneity is not a QM-specific problem. It is an inheritance
from classical physics that QM took over unchanged.

In Newtonian mechanics, gravity acts instantaneously over arbitrary
distances. The Coulomb potential is instantaneous. Lagrangian and
Hamiltonian mechanics describe the state $(q(t),p(t))$ at a fixed
$t$ — the calculation is a timeless algebraic evaluation over the
instantaneous state. Nobody asks how long this calculation
``takes'', because calculations in this language have no duration.
The time $t$ runs in the background as an external parameter —
the physics resides in the timeless structure of the equations.

This is the same structure as in QM. Pauli only made explicit what
was tacit in the classical framework.

\subsection{The hidden inconsistency of QM}

QM inherits this instantaneity — and adds a new tension:

\textbf{The state $\psi(t)$ is instantaneous.} It is a timeless
mathematical quantity at a fixed $t$ — no duration, no process.

\textbf{Evaluating $|\psi(t)|^2$ requires time.}
$|\psi(t)|^2$ is a probability density — to verify it one needs
many measurements, hence real physical time. The statistics that
confirm the state are distributed over a temporal process. The
formalism, however, describes only the state at one moment.

This is the tacit inconsistency: \emph{the formalism is
instantaneous, but its verification is temporally extended.}
The time required for probabilistic evaluation stands outside the
formalism — it is neither operator nor variable, but what the
experimenter expends in order to know the state at all.

\subsection{Consequence for superposition}

Superposition $c_1\ket{\psi_1}+c_2\ket{\psi_2}$ is therefore
doubly timeless: first as a state (no time operator, no intrinsic
``when''), second as a statement (probabilistic, requires time for
verification, which lies outside the formalism). What ``exists'' in
superposition can only be confirmed through repeated measurements
over real time — and each individual measurement collapses the
state, thus destroying the superposition.

In FFGFT: the period $\tilde{T}_i=1/m_i$ gives the mode an
intrinsic time scale. Superposition is not timeless — it has a
geometrically defined scale on which it is stable or unstable.

\section{The Absence of Time in QM — an Underestimated Consequence}

\subsection{The Pauli theorem and its consequences}

Wolfgang Pauli showed in 1933 that for a Hamiltonian with a spectrum
bounded from below there can be no self-adjoint time operator
canonically conjugate to $\hat{H}$ (Doc.~307, Doc.~306). The
consequence was tacit but far-reaching: time was kept outside the
Hilbert space and treated as a classical external parameter.

This is not merely a technical decision. It means:
\textbf{time is not an observable in QM.} One cannot ask ``when is
the system in state $\psi$?'' and obtain an answer from the formalism
— $t$ must be supplied from outside.

\subsection{What is rarely stated in textbooks}

Most textbooks introduce time as a parameter without emphasising what
the absence of a time operator means. It means concretely:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Superposition $c_1\ket{\psi_1}+c_2\ket{\psi_2}$ is a state
    \emph{without time structure} — there is no intrinsic ``clock''
    saying how long the system remains in this superposition.
  \item Entanglement between two particles is \emph{timeless} — the
    joint state has no built-in notion of simultaneity.
  \item Collapse is \emph{without duration} — it ``happens'' in the
    external parameter $t$, but $t$ itself is not part of the collapse
    process.
\end{itemize}

This is not a failure of QM — it is a structural property that is
rarely made explicit.

\section{Three Consequences in Detail}

\subsection{Superposition is timeless}

An electron in the state
$\ket{\psi} = c_\uparrow\ket{\uparrow} + c_\downarrow\ket{\downarrow}$
has no intrinsic time scale. The question ``how long is it in this
superposition?'' is not answerable within the QM formalism — one
would need a time operator, which does not exist. What does exist is
the coherence time $T_2$ — but that is a phenomenological quantity
describing interaction with the environment, not an intrinsic
property of the state.

In FFGFT this is different: every mode $\mathcal{T}_i=(n_i,l_i,j_i)$
carries $\tilde{T}_i=1/m_i$ as a geometric quantity. A state
\emph{is} its time scale — not as the duration of a superposition,
but as a structural property of the mode.

\subsection{Entanglement without simultaneity}

Two entangled particles share a joint state
$\ket{\Psi}\in\mathcal{H}_1\otimes\mathcal{H}_2$. This joint state
has no built-in time structure. The question of the simultaneity of
a measurement on both particles is therefore not a QM question but a
classical question about the external parameter $t$ — and hence a
relativistic question (relativity of simultaneity).

This is the deepest reason why QM is compatible with Lorentz
invariance: because ``simultaneity'' stands outside the formalism
and can therefore be transformed by the Lorentz group without
touching the QM formalism.

In FFGFT: if two different modes with $m_1\neq m_2$ were entangled,
they would carry different $\tilde{T}_1\neq\tilde{T}_2$. A
cross-mode entanglement therefore generates incompatible time scales
— structurally more problematic than in QM, where this conflict does
not arise because $t$ stands outside.

\subsection{Collapse without duration}

The measurement process in QM describes a transition
$\ket{\psi}\to\ket{\psi_k}$ (projection onto an eigenstate). This
transition has \emph{no duration} in the QM formalism — it is
instantaneous in the parameter $t$. How long the collapse takes is a
question that lies outside standard QM (it is discussed in theories
of the measurement process, decoherence, or quantum gravity).

This is not an inconsistency but a direct consequence of the absence
of the time operator: without $\hat{t}$ there is no observable
``collapse duration''.

In FFGFT the type-I step (measurement act, Doc.~285/333) is not
instantaneous in the abstract sense but a geometric transition —
the mass circle $S^1_m$ becomes the time axis $\mathbb{R}_t$, the
winding number is discarded. This is a loss of information that has
a natural ``direction''.

\section{What $\tilde{T}\cdot m = 1$ Changes Structurally}

\subsection{Time as a property of the mode, not a parameter}

The fundamental relation $\tilde{T}\cdot m=1$ (Doc.~285, 306, 307)
sets time not as an external parameter but as a geometric quantity
of every mode: the period $R_m=\hbar/(mc^2)$ is intrinsic — it
belongs to the particle as its mass does.

This has three direct consequences for the points above:

\textbf{Superposition:} A superposition within one mode —
intermediate torsion (Doc.~174) — has a natural time scale
$\tilde{T}_i$. Superposition \emph{across} different modes would
overlay incompatible time scales $\tilde{T}_i\neq\tilde{T}_j$ —
this is the structural reason why cross-mode superpositions are
problematic in FFGFT (Doc.~333).

\textbf{Entanglement:} Within one mode, simultaneity is defined by
$\tilde{T}$. Across modes there is no shared time concept — similar
to QM, but for a different reason: not because $t$ stands outside,
but because different modes carry different geometric time scales.

\textbf{Collapse (Type I):} The measurement act (Doc.~285) has a
natural direction — lossy from $S^1_m$ to $\mathbb{R}_t$ — and a
natural asymmetry: the reverse direction $\mathbb{R}_t\to S^1_m$
requires the discarded winding number. The arrow of time is thereby
not an external additional assumption but a consequence of the
geometry of measurement.

\subsection{Pauli theorem and compact time}

The Pauli theorem applies to an \emph{open} time parameter conjugate
to a Hamiltonian bounded from below. For a \emph{compact} time
dimension the argument does not apply (Doc.~307): compact time
behaves like an angle variable, its conjugate has a discrete spectrum
(winding numbers), and the Pauli condition is not met.

$\tilde{T}\cdot m=1$ realises exactly this situation: time is
compact, the conjugate spectrum is the discrete mass ladder. The
theorem does not exclude this case — it virtually forces it as the
only consistent way to integrate time into the Hilbert space.
