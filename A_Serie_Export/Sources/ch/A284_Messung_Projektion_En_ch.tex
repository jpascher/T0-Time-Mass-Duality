\section{What this is about}

\textbf{[STIPULATION]} In standard quantum mechanics the measurement
process is the most conceptually unsatisfying element: a unified,
deterministic evolution of the wave function breaks down at measurement,
a probabilistic outcome appears, and two distant particles seem to
know about each other instantaneously. Three decades after Bell
violation, the question of what physically happens remains open.

\textbf{[B]} FFGFT already has the elements of an answer distributed
across other documents: A090 (projection chain, Type I/II/III), A070
(measurement as pole transition, Born rule from phase ignorance), A160
(Bell correlations as topological connection), A030 (winding classes
without location). What is missing is the closed chain of argument.
This document draws it.

\section{Light rays as observer abstraction --- the EM field as carrier}
\label{sec:lichtstrahlen}

\subsection{What a light ray actually is}

\textbf{[S]} A light ray is not a physical quantity. It is a
\emph{geometric abstraction} distilled from the EM field under specific
conditions --- namely when the wavelength $\lambda$ is small compared to
all relevant geometric dimensions (apertures, objects, observer distance).
This condition is \emph{ray optics as a limiting case of wave optics} ---
mathematically: $\lambda \to 0$ in the wave equation yields the eikonal
equation, from which rays follow as normal trajectories of phase surfaces.

\textbf{[B]} The EM field is the carrier. It exists and evolves according
to Maxwell's equations, entirely independent of observers. The light ray
appears only when an observer or detector arrangement makes a position
measurement: the detector responds to the field at its location and
\emph{constructs} backwards a ray as the geometric line connecting source
and detector. The ray is the result of this construction --- not its
precursor.

\textbf{[B]} This is not new --- it is fully present in classical wave
optics. What FFGFT adds: field evolution on $T^4$ has no locations in the
standard sense. A photon has no trajectory; it has a mode structure on the
torus. What we observe as a ``light path'' is the statistical accumulation
of detection events in a spatial region --- a projection statistic, not a
physical trajectory (A090, Type-II projection).

\subsection{Why it is so hard to let go of the light-path picture}

\textbf{[S]} The light-path picture sits deep for four reasons:

\textbf{(1) Evolutionary.} The human brain is optimised for causality
through spatial transfer: stone flies, hits, acts. ``Cause before effect,
carrier connects, distance separates'' --- these heuristics were
survival-relevant for $10^5$ years. They sit deeper than any theory.

\textbf{(2) Linguistic.} Every natural language has subjects performing
predicates: ``Light goes from A to B.'' The idea that a field exists and
an observer reacts locally --- without ``something'' travelling from A
to B --- is not grammatically natural. Even physically correct sentences
sound like negations, not positive statements.

\textbf{(3) Historical.} Newton thought in corpuscles --- light particles
on trajectories. Maxwell solved this: fields instead of trajectories. But
then photons arrived, and everyday physics regressed to the trajectory
picture: ``The photon goes through the double slit.'' Wave optics became
a calculation method, the particle trajectory became the intuition. This
regression is deeply embedded in physics education.

\textbf{(4) Visibility only via detours.} Light rays become visible only
through intermediaries: dust, smoke, droplets --- scattering centres that
locally absorb and re-emit the field. What we see in a searchlight beam
is millions of scattering events, each at a different location. The
``beam'' is the statistical line of these events. Without scatterers the
EM field is invisible --- physically real, but not an object of observation.

\subsection{Why do lenses work then?}

\textbf{[B]} The lens question is the most precise test of the thesis.
If light rays are abstractions and the EM field is the carrier --- why
does a lens focus?

The answer lies in two layers:

\textbf{Layer 1 --- Wave optics (complete description):}
The EM field encounters the glass medium of the lens. Inside the glass
the phase velocity is $v = c/n$, less than in vacuum. The wave front ---
a surface of equal phase --- slows down more in the thicker part of the
lens than in the thinner part. After passing through, the wave front is
no longer flat but curved: it converges. The field behind develops toward
a focal-point structure --- a region of maximum field intensity. No ray
is needed: Maxwell's equations in the inhomogeneous medium describe
everything completely.

\textbf{Layer 2 --- Ray optics (limiting-case description):}
In the limit $\lambda \ll f$ (wavelength $\ll$ focal length), Snell's law
holds: $n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$. Rays are now the normal
trajectories of wave fronts --- they describe \emph{where the field
concentrates}, not where a particle comes from. The lens does not deflect
bodies; it reshapes phase surfaces.

\textbf{[B]} The decisive point: the lens acts on the field, not on rays.
The ray is a useful geometric description of the result --- but the field
is the cause. An observer behind the lens sees the image because the field
has high intensity at the detector location. The observer \emph{constructs}
from this a ray path.

\textbf{[S]} In the FFGFT reading: the photon is a field mode on $T^4$.
It has no trajectory. The lens modifies the local metric of the EM field
--- it changes the phase structure of the mode. Behind the lens the
probability of detecting the photon at the focal point is maximal. The
focal point is not a station on a trajectory but a maximum of the
projection probability. That lenses ``work'' is a statement about the
distribution of Type-II projection events in space --- not about trajectories.

\subsection{The connection to the Bell question}

\textbf{[B]} The connection to the instantaneity question is direct:
whoever asks ``how can the measurement at A instantaneously affect B?''
is thinking in light paths. The light path is the implicit assumption
that every effect has a spatial carrier that moves from A to B.

But if the EM field is the carrier --- and the field exists globally on
$T^4$ --- then there is no ``from A to B''. The field is already everywhere.
The measurement at A is a local interaction with the global field. The
correlation at B is the response of the same global field at a different
projection point. No carrier needs to travel.

\textbf{[S]} The lens example makes this tangible: when light passes
through a lens and arrives at the focal point, no one asks ``which ray
caused the focal point?'' That is the wrong question. The field has the
phase structure that leads to this focal point. Likewise: when an
entangled photon pair is measured, FFGFT does not ask ``which signal
ran from A to B?'' The field has the mode structure that leads to these
correlations.

\section{What measurement is in FFGFT}

\subsection{Measurement is a Type-II projection}

\textbf{[B]} In the projection chain (A090) there are three qualitatively
distinct operations: Type~I (duality decompactification, nearly reversible),
Type~II (geometric projection, lossy, irreversible), Type~III
(representational translation, bijective).

\textbf{[B]} A measurement is a Type-II operation. It collapses a
dimension of the state space --- it projects the global state on $T^4$
onto a lower-dimensional subspace. The kernel of this projection
$\ker(\pi) = \mathbb{R}$ contains information no longer present in the
image (A090). This lost information is not gone --- it is deposited in
the measuring apparatus, which thereby becomes part of the total system.

\textbf{[B]} Three consequences follow immediately:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Measurement acts globally.} The $T^4$ is boundary-free
    ($\partial T^4 = \emptyset$, A030) and carries global eigenmodes
    without a distinguished location. A projection at one projection
    point changes the global mode content --- there is no local measurement
    that leaves the rest of the system untouched.
  \item \textbf{The apparatus becomes part of the system.} What was
    previously system state + apparatus state is afterwards a joint
    state. The boundary is not a physical boundary but a model boundary
    (interface definition).
  \item \textbf{Repetition changes the system.} Every subsequent
    measurement encounters a different global state than the first. The
    torus \enquote{knows} about the first measurement --- not through
    a signal, but because its global mode content has changed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The geometric reading of collapse}

\textbf{[S]} In standard QM the wave function collapses onto an
eigenstate at measurement. In the FFGFT reading (A070) this is a
deterministic motion in mode space: the $(z,r,\theta)$ point is
driven to the nearest stable extreme ($z=+1$ or $z=-1$). This is
not an instantaneous action at a distance but a local geometric motion
at the projection point. The \enquote{collapse} is the Type-II
projection step itself.

\textbf{[B]} What is lost in the process is the phase $\theta$ ---
the azimuthal information of the mode. It is no longer accessible in
the image after measurement, but it is not destroyed: it is encoded
in the measuring apparatus (Type-II loss).

\section{Where probabilism comes from}

\subsection{Three possible explanations}

There are three structurally distinct answers to the question of why
quantum measurements yield probabilistic outcomes:

\textbf{Standard reading (ontological randomness):} $|\psi|^2$ is an
ontological probability. The outcome is fundamentally random, not
determined by hidden variables (Bell, Kochen-Specker).

\textbf{Hidden-variable reading (local):} The outcome is determined
by hidden local parameters. This reading is experimentally excluded by Bell.

\textbf{FFGFT reading (projection loss):} The outcome is determined by
the deterministic field equations on $T^4$, but the phase $\theta$ is
not accessible to the observer. Stochasticity arises at the measuring
apparatus (the Type-II projection), not in the natural process.

\subsection{Why the FFGFT reading does not violate Bell's theorem}

\textbf{[B]} Bell excludes \emph{local} hidden variables on the projected
$\mathbb{R}^3$. The phase $\theta$ on $T^4$ is not a local hidden
variable in the sense of the theorem --- it is a global feature of the
torus mode, not a parameter sitting at a spatial location. Bell's proof
assumes a factorisation of the hidden-variable density across the spatial
separation. This factorisation does not hold on $T^4$ because locations
on $T^4$ are defined by their winding classes, not by spatial coordinates
(A030).

\textbf{[B]} The quantitative form: the Born rule
\[
  P_\text{FFGFT}(z=+1) = \frac{1+z}{2} = |\alpha|^2 = P_\text{QM}(0)
\]
follows from the uniform distribution over $\theta \in [0,2\pi)$ at large
statistics (A070). Probabilism is the ensemble average over all phase
states --- not fundamental randomness, but averaging over what the
embedded observer cannot see.

\subsection{Born rule as projection statistics}

\textbf{[S]} The structural thesis: the Born rule $P = |\psi|^2$ is
the statistics of Type-II information loss. In a projection
$\pi: \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$ the information of the lost
dimension distributes over the ensemble of repeated measurements. The
quadratic form arises because the norm in Hilbert space is Euclidean
and the projection acts isometrically on the subspace.

\textbf{[H]} The quantitative connection between Type-II loss and the
exact form of the Born rule ($|\psi|^2$ rather than $|\psi|$ or
$|\psi|^3$) is not worked out. That is the genuine open point --- not
whether but precisely how the projection statistics enforces the
quadratic form.

\section{Three levels of non-locality}

\textbf{[B]} The instantaneity question in Bell tests has three levels
that must be kept strictly separate. All three are consistently
resolvable in the FFGFT framework --- in different ways.

\subsection{Level 1 --- Torus picture: topological neighbourhood}

\textbf{[STIPULATION]} (A160) Bell correlations on $T^4$ are a
topological phenomenon. What appears in the projected $\mathbb{R}^3$
as action at a distance are points connected by closed paths in the
compact picture --- topological neighbours.

\textbf{[B]} For terrestrial separations $d \ll L^*$: both measurement
points A and B lie on the same torus sheet. The $T^4$ with the
closure scale $L^* = 4\bar\lambda_e/\xi_0^{10} \approx 1{.}3 \times
10^{26}$\,m is practically flat on terrestrial scales ($d \sim 10^6$\,m).
\emph{But:} the topological connection runs not via the Euclidean
distance but via the winding class. An entangled pair shares the same
winding class --- that is the meaning of the topological connection,
independent of the projected separation.

\subsection{Level 2 --- Field picture: global eigenmode}

\textbf{[B]} (A030) Winding classes and eigenmodes have no location.
An eigenmode is global --- it is a property of the entire torus, not
of a region. An entangled pair is not a pair of separate objects with
a connection but \emph{one} object with two projection sites (A160).

\textbf{[B]} It follows immediately: nothing propagates from A to B.
The correlation is identity, not transmission. Signal-freedom is not
an additional condition that must be imposed --- it follows from the
construction.

\subsection{Level 3 --- Measurement process: global projection}

\textbf{[S]} This is the deepest level, not explicitly stated in the
previous documents.

The measurement at A is not a local disturbance that subsequently
propagates to B. It is a Type-II projection of the \emph{global}
state on $T^4$. This projection changes the global mode content ---
the joint state of the total system to which both projection sites
A and B belong. What appears at B as an \enquote{instantaneous effect}
is not an effect coming from A, but the changed boundary condition of
the global state from which B reads off.

\textbf{[S]} Put differently: measurement at A reads the global mode
and modifies it in doing so. The next measurement at B encounters an
already modified global mode --- not because a signal travelled from A
to B, but because both are part of the same global system.

\subsection{Why evaluation remains probabilistic in principle}

\textbf{[B]} All three levels converge on the same point.
Evaluation is probabilistic because:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item Every single measurement is a Type-II projection (loss of
    the phase $\theta$).
  \item The global mode is modified after every measurement ---
    the starting state is never exactly the same a second time.
  \item The ensemble over many measurements delivers the phase
    averaging from which the Born rule follows.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{[B]} A Bell correlation cannot be read from a single measurement.
An ensemble is always required. The ensemble is the statistics of the
projection --- and the projection is global. Evaluation is therefore
probabilistic in principle, not for convenience, but because the
single measurement process systematically hides the information.

\section{Field travel time, not path travel time --- the measurable delay}

\subsection{The crucial distinction}

\textbf{[B]} There are two fundamentally different ways in which a delay
$\Delta t = d/c$ can arise:

\textbf{Path travel time:} An object or signal carrier moves from point
A to point B. There is \emph{something} that traverses the path. The
detector at B registers this something when it arrives. This is the
intuitive picture underlying the concept of the light ray.

\textbf{Field travel time:} The field already exists everywhere. At
point A something happens --- a measurement, a disturbance, an
interaction --- and this \emph{change in field state} spreads as a
wave front. The detector at B does not register an arriving object
but a changed field configuration at its location.

\textbf{[B]} The mathematical expression is identical for both cases
--- $\delta(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'| - c(t-t'))$ --- but the physical
interpretation is fundamentally different. In the Green's function
(Doc.~131):
\[
  G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}',t-t')
  = \theta(t-t') \cdot \frac{\delta(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'| - c(t-t'))}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|}
\]
$\mathbf{x}'$ is the \emph{source point of the disturbance} and
$\mathbf{x}$ the \emph{observation point}. The $\delta$ function says:
the change reaches the observation point exactly when
$|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'| = c(t-t')$. What propagates is the
\emph{wave front of the field change} --- not a carrier.

\subsection{What propagates in a Bell measurement}

\textbf{[S]} At the moment of measurement at detector A the following
happens in field terms:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item The global EM field (the entangled state) interacts with the
    measuring apparatus at location A. This is a local interaction ---
    not a global event.
  \item Through this interaction the field state at A changes: phase,
    amplitude, mode occupation are modified. This is a \emph{local
    field change}.
  \item This local field change spreads as a retarded wave --- with
    the Green's function $G(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_A, t-t_A)$. It
    reaches every point $\mathbf{x}$ at time $t_A + |\mathbf{x} -
    \mathbf{x}_A|/c$.
  \item Detector B at location $\mathbf{x}_B$ registers the changed
    field configuration at time $t_A + d_{AB}/c$ --- not because a
    signal travelled from A to B, but because the field change has
    reached it.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{[B]} This is field travel time, not path travel time. The
distinction is physically real:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item With path travel time: B must \emph{wait} until the signal
    arrives. Before that, B \emph{knows nothing}.
  \item With field travel time: B interacts with the global field
    the whole time. Before, B responds to the undisturbed field state.
    After, B responds to the field state modified by the A measurement.
    The transition occurs when the wave front arrives.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{[B]} No information has been transmitted from A to B. The
modified field configuration at B is not a message from A --- it is
the natural evolution of the global field after the A interaction.
Since B did not know the unmodified field state anyway (Type-II
projection, phase $\theta$ unknown), B cannot extract information
about the A measurement from the modified field state either ---
except by comparison with A via a classical channel.

\subsection{The $\xi$ delay as a field phase offset}

\textbf{[K]} (A165) The FFGFT prediction:
\[
  \Delta t_\xi = \xi \cdot \frac{d}{c} \approx 445\,\text{ns}
  \quad (d = 1000\,\text{km})
\]

\textbf{[S]} This delay is \emph{not} a path travel time and
\emph{not} an additional signal transmission. It is a \emph{phase
offset in the field wave front} arising from the fractal torus geometry.

Precisely: the global field on $T^4$ is not completely flat ---
on terrestrial scales there is a residue of compactness (A090). This
residue generates a geometric phase shift of the field modes. When the
wave front of the field change spreads out from A, it carries this
phase offset --- not because it takes a detour, but because the field
geometry on the torus deviates slightly from the Euclidean.

\textbf{[S]} This means: what detector B measures is the field amplitude
with a phase offset $\xi \cdot d/c$ relative to what B would measure
without this geometric effect. The 445\,ns is the time over which this
phase offset shifts the correlation statistics --- not the travel time
of a signal.

\textbf{[B]} Whether this effect is measurable depends on whether one
can measure the correlation statistics with a time resolution
$\ll 445$\,ns relative to a common time reference Q. Measuring the
path travel time (signal A$\to$B) would be conceptually wrong. Measuring
the field travel time (change in correlation statistics relative to
$t_Q + d_B/c$) is the right question.

\subsection{Why it has never been measured}

Previous Bell experiments fall into four generations.
None of them posed the question that would be decisive for the FFGFT prediction.

\textbf{Generation~1: Demonstrating Bell violation.}
Aspect, Dalibard and Roger (1982) \cite{Aspect1982} demonstrated Bell violation
with time-varying analysers for the first time. The goal was to show $S > 2$.
Timing information was used only to identify coincidences (windows $\sim \mu$s),
not to resolve the correlation structure in time.

\textbf{Generation~2: Closing the locality loophole.}
Weihs, Jennewein, Simon, Weinfurter and Zeilinger (1998) \cite{Weihs1998}
closed the locality loophole via fast random choice of measurement setting
($\tau_\text{decision} \ll d/c$). Time resolution ($\sim 1$\,ns) was
sufficient for that purpose, but the experiment measured A against B ---
not relative to the source point Q.

\textbf{Generation~3: Loophole-free Bell tests.}
Three independent groups (Hensen et al.\ 2015 \cite{Hensen2015};
Giustina et al.\ 2015 \cite{Giustina2015}; Shalm et al.\ 2015 \cite{Shalm2015})
simultaneously closed locality and detection loopholes.
Time resolution: $\sim 1$\,ns, separation $d \sim 1$\,km.
For $d = 1$\,km FFGFT would predict $\Delta t = \xi \cdot d/c \approx 0{.}44$\,ns
--- just below the time resolution of these experiments.
But again the question was: does $S$ violate the classical bound?
Not: is there a delay in the correlation structure?

\textbf{Generation~4: Cosmic separations.}
Yin et al.\ (2017) \cite{Yin2017} demonstrated Bell violation with the Micius
satellite over $\sim 1200$\,km. For this distance FFGFT would predict
$\Delta t \approx 530$\,ns --- well above the detectability threshold of
atomic clocks ($\sim 10$\,ns). But Micius had \emph{no} precise source-point
reference Q. Timestamps were used for coincidence windows, not for an
analysis of the causal propagation structure. Moreover Micius did not have
three synchronised clocks (Q, A, B) with a precise common time base.

\textbf{The structural problem.}
All four generations measure the same thing: whether the correlation statistics
over many events violates the classical bound. That is an ensemble question.
The FFGFT prediction of the 445\,ns delay is instead a question about the
\emph{temporal structure} of the field modification per individual event ---
statistically extracted, but conceptually distinct.

An FFGFT test would require:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item A precisely time-stamped source point Q (pair generation time to
    $\ll 1$\,ns accuracy).
  \item Three synchronised atomic clocks: Q, A, B (common time base to
    $\sim 1$\,ns over $d \sim 1000$\,km).
  \item A statistical analysis extracting the delay $\Delta t$ of the
    correlation change at B relative to $t_Q + d_B/c$ --- not relative
    to $t_A$.
  \item Sufficient event count to extract $\Delta t \approx 445$\,ns
    from the noise (shot noise $\sim 1/\sqrt{N}$).
\end{enumerate}

No one has performed this experiment because the question ---
is there a $\xi$-scaled delay in the propagation of the field modification?
--- makes no sense in the standard QM framework: in the standard reading
nothing propagates; collapse is instantaneous. The question is only
meaningful under a theory that assumes a causal field structure behind
the correlations.

\section{Open question: are A and B the same field point?}
\label{sec:identischer_punkt}

\subsection{The question}

\textbf{[H]} In the projected $\mathbb{R}^3$ two detectors A and B
are separated by $d = 1000$\,km. In the torus picture:
\[
  \mathbf{x}_A \equiv \mathbf{x}_B \pmod{L^*}
\]
Since $L^* = 4\bar\lambda_e/\xi_0^{10} \approx 1{.}3 \times 10^{26}$\,m,
the terrestrial separation $d \sim 10^6$\,m is negligible:
$d/L^* \approx 10^{-20}$. In the torus picture A and B lie in the
same fundamental domain --- practically at the same location.

\textbf{[H]} The deeper question is not metric but
\emph{field-ontological}: are A and B two different points for the
entangled field --- or the \emph{same} field point, read out at two
projection sites?

The corpus has the elements of the question (A030: winding classes
have no location; A090: identification $t \sim t + R_m$; A160:
entangled pair is one object with two projection images) but does not
state the question explicitly and gives no answer. This is a genuine
gap, not a knowledge gap.

\subsection{Three structurally distinct possibilities}

\textbf{[H]} There are three answers that are not equivalent:

\textbf{Possibility~1 --- two points, topologically connected [S]:}
A and B are different points in the torus picture, but connected by
the same winding class. The entangled pair is a mode that encompasses
both points. The field modification after the A measurement propagates
as a wave front to B. This implies: the $\xi$ delay
$\Delta t = \xi \cdot d/c \approx 445$\,ns is measurable
(§\,\ref{sec:lichtstrahlen}).

\textbf{Possibility~2 --- identical point in the fundamental domain [H]:}
Since $d \ll L^*$, A and B are practically the same point in the
fundamental domain of the torus: $\mathbf{x}_A \approx \mathbf{x}_B$.
The entangled mode is fully local within the fundamental domain ---
there is no separation between them in the torus picture. The
correlation is instantaneous in the torus picture; the 445\,ns is
an artefact of projection.

\subsection{What the corpus already implies}

\textbf{[B]} A030 (winding classes without location) and A090
(identification $t \sim t + R_m$) together provide a structure in
which ``location'' for field modes is not a primary concept. This
speaks for Possibility~2 or~3 rather than~1.

\textbf{[B]} A160 says the entangled pair is ``one object with two
projection images''. If it is \emph{one} object, it has \emph{one}
field configuration in the torus picture --- not two at different
locations. The two projection images only arise through the Type-II
projection (A090) onto $\mathbb{R}^3$. This speaks for Possibility~3.

\textbf{[S]} If Possibility~3 is correct, this has a strong
consequence: the question ``why is the correlation instantaneous?''
is then not a question about propagation speed --- it is a question
about why we take the projection of one object at two sites to be
two measurements. The answer is not physics but epistemology: the
projection image deceives us about the number of objects.

\subsection{Consequence for the 445-ns prediction}

\textbf{[H]} The prediction $\Delta t = \xi \cdot d/c$ from A165
assumes Possibility~1. It is correct if A and B are different field
points in the torus picture, between which a field modification can
propagate.

If instead Possibility~2 or~3 holds, the prediction shifts
conceptually: the effect then does not exist as field travel time
between A and B, but as a phase offset in the projection itself ---
measurable as a timing asymmetry in the correlation statistics, but
with a different origin.

\textbf{[H]} This question cannot currently be decided within the
FFGFT corpus. It requires a precise formulation of what ``location
in the field'' means for a mode structure on $T^4$ --- a bridge
between the topological structure of A030 and the measurement process
of A090 that has not yet been built.

Until clarified, A165 remains valid as a prediction --- under the
explicit assumption of Possibility~1. The assumption is to be
declared, not concealed.

\section{Open questions}

\textbf{[H]} Three genuine open points:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Born rule from Type-II loss:} The quantitative
    derivation of the quadratic form $P = |\psi|^2$ from projection
    loss is not worked out. Why square and not absolute value or higher
    power? The isometric property of the Hilbert-space projection is
    the candidate (A090), but the proof is missing.
  \item \textbf{Path assignment for entanglement:} Which closed paths
    on $T^4$ correspond to which concrete entangled states (A160, [S]).
    Without this assignment the topological thesis remains a structural
    statement, not an operational criterion.
  \item \textbf{Global mode and local measurement:} How precisely does
    a Type-II projection at one projection point modify the global mode
    content? The qualitative mechanism is clear (Type-II loss); the
    quantitative field equation for the global back-action is not worked out.
\end{enumerate}
